# Elite Dispatcher Academy — Module 07 Instructor Answer Guide

**Rate Mathematics & Profitability**

Instructor use only. Keep this guide separate from the student edition.

## Knowledge Check — Answer Key

### Part A

1. **b** — Loaded RPM = gross load revenue ÷ loaded miles.
2. **b** — Effective RPM = total gross load revenue ÷ total trip miles.
3. **b** — Total trip miles = origin deadhead + loaded miles + relevant destination/repositioning deadhead.
4. **b** — Contribution margin is applicable revenue minus included variable costs, explicitly stated — not net profit, not cash flow, and not inclusive of driver pay/insurance/equipment.
5. **c** — Break-even depends on the carrier's own complete cost structure and accounting methodology.

### Part B

6. **False** — Section 7 (Loaded Mile Blindness) / Worked Example 2. A higher loaded RPM can still result in a lower effective RPM and lower dollars after fuel once deadhead is included.
7. **False** — Section 11. Contribution margin as taught here excludes driver pay, maintenance, insurance, equipment, and other fixed/carrier-specific costs — it is not net profit.
8. **False** — Section 13. Detention terms are agreement-specific and must be verified from the rate confirmation and documentation, never assumed as a standard threshold or rate.
9. **False** — Section 12. National averages provide general context only; they do not represent any specific carrier's break-even figure.
10. **False** — Section 9. Fuel prices change continuously; use a current sourced figure (e.g., EIA) rather than a hard-coded price.

### Part C

11. **A** — Zero relevant deadhead means loaded and effective RPM will be identical or very close.
12. **B** — 250 miles of deadhead against only 300 loaded miles is a large deadhead-to-loaded ratio; effective RPM will drop meaningfully below loaded RPM.
13. **C** — Without deadhead information, the relationship between loaded RPM and effective RPM cannot be classified.
14. **A** — 40 miles of deadhead against 700 loaded miles is a small ratio; loaded and effective RPM will be close, though not identical.

### Part D — Sample Reasoning

15. Loaded RPM only reflects revenue over the paid miles and ignores deadhead entirely. Effective RPM divides the same revenue over all miles actually driven, including deadhead, giving a more complete picture of what the load pays per mile of the truck's time. A load can look strong on loaded RPM and weak on effective RPM once deadhead is heavy.

16. Contribution margin as calculated in this module only subtracts the specific variable costs that were included in that calculation (for example, fuel, tolls, dispatcher fee, factoring fee). It has not accounted for driver compensation, maintenance, insurance, equipment payments, permits, taxes, or administrative costs — all of which affect the carrier's actual profit. Calling it "profit" overstates how much is genuinely left over.

17. Accessorial amounts (detention, TONU, layover, lumper reimbursement, extra stops) are set by the specific rate confirmation, broker/carrier agreement, or dispatcher agreement for that transaction — they are not standardized across the industry. Teaching a fixed number would give students false confidence and lead them to expect payment that may not actually be owed, or to miss payment they're actually entitled to under different terms.

18. Using a memorized "typical" percentage or price instead of the actual agreement or a current source risks giving the carrier inaccurate numbers. Dispatcher fees and factoring fees come from the specific signed agreements in place; fuel prices change over time and by region. A dispatcher who defaults to assumed figures rather than verified ones is doing the same kind of unsupported guessing this academy trains students to avoid in every other module.

19. A carrier's break-even cost per mile is specific to that carrier's actual cost structure, equipment, driver pay arrangement, and accounting methodology. A national average operating-cost figure (such as those published by industry research organizations) reflects an aggregate across many different carriers and operating models, and can vary significantly from any individual carrier's real number. The national figure is useful for general industry context; it is not a substitute for that specific carrier's own numbers.

20. Load A had a higher loaded RPM ($3.00 vs. $2.50) because it was calculated only over the 400 loaded miles. But Load A also had 200 miles of deadhead, bringing its total trip miles to 600 — the same total mileage the fuel cost was calculated against. Load B had no deadhead at all, so all of its 460 miles were productive miles, and its lower revenue was spread over fewer total miles and required less fuel. The result is that Load B left more money after fuel ($881.67) than Load A ($850), despite Load A's higher headline rate — because Load A's advertised rate didn't reflect the unpaid miles it also required.

## Student Practice Exercise Answers (Section 20)

1. Total trip miles = 60 + 280 + 0 = **340**. Loaded RPM = $980 ÷ 280 = **$3.50**. Effective RPM = $980 ÷ 340 ≈ **$2.88**.
2. Estimated gallons = 340 ÷ 6.8 = **50 gallons**. Estimated fuel cost = 50 × $3.65 = **$182.50**.
3. Dispatcher fee = $980 × 6% = **$58.80**. Included variable costs = $182.50 (fuel) + $58.80 (dispatcher) = **$241.30** (no tolls, no factoring fee in this exercise). Estimated contribution margin = $980 − $241.30 = **$738.70**. State explicitly: this includes only fuel and dispatcher fee — not driver pay, maintenance, insurance, equipment, or other carrier costs.
4. Load X: total miles = 30 + 500 = 530; effective RPM = $1,300 ÷ 530 ≈ **$2.45**. Load Y: total miles = 480 (no deadhead); effective RPM = $1,150 ÷ 480 ≈ **$2.40**. Load X has the (slightly) higher effective RPM in this exercise — students should show both calculations, not just state the answer.
5. The proposed load's effective RPM ($1.68) is below the carrier's stated all-in cost per mile ($1.75). Based on the break-even concept in Section 12, this suggests the load may not cover the carrier's costs at the revenue level given — but a dispatcher should not declare the load unprofitable outright; they should present this comparison to the carrier and let the carrier weigh it against their own full picture (which may include factors, like next-load positioning, that the effective-RPM number alone doesn't capture).

## Scenario-Based Exercise Answer (Section 22)

**Load 1:** Total miles = 90 + 550 + 20 = 660. Loaded RPM = $1,600 ÷ 550 ≈ $2.91. Effective RPM = $1,600 ÷ 660 ≈ $2.42. Fuel = 660 ÷ 6.5 ≈ 101.54 gal × $3.80 ≈ $385.85. Dispatcher fee = $1,600 × 5% = $80. Contribution margin (fuel + dispatcher only) = $1,600 − ($385.85 + $80) = **$1,134.15**.

**Load 2:** Total miles = 15 + 520 = 535. Loaded RPM = $1,480 ÷ 520 ≈ $2.85. Effective RPM = $1,480 ÷ 535 ≈ $2.77. Fuel = 535 ÷ 6.5 ≈ 82.31 gal × $3.80 ≈ $312.77. Dispatcher fee = $1,480 × 5% = $74. Factoring fee = $1,480 × 2.5% = $37. Contribution margin (fuel + dispatcher + factoring) = $1,480 − ($312.77 + $74 + $37) = **$1,056.23**.

**Expected reasoning:** Load 2 has a notably higher effective RPM ($2.77 vs. $2.42) because of its minimal deadhead, but Load 1 shows a higher estimated contribution margin in dollar terms in this exercise, partly because it wasn't charged a factoring fee in the given assumptions. A strong answer should present both loads' full numbers to the carrier rather than declaring a single "winner," and should explicitly note that the two contribution-margin figures used slightly different included-cost categories (Load 2 included a factoring fee that Load 1 did not) — an important detail to flag rather than bury, since it affects a clean side-by-side comparison.

## Instructor Safety / Publication Controls

### No Universal Accessorial Figures
Do not teach fixed detention thresholds, TONU amounts, layover pay, or lumper-reimbursement rules as industry standards. All accessorial teaching must route back to Section 13's verification list (rate confirmation, agreement, documentation).

### No Unsourced "Standard Fees"
Dispatcher fee, factoring fee, and quick-pay percentages used in this module (5%, 3%, 2.5%, 6%, etc.) are fictional instructional assumptions only, clearly labeled as such throughout. Do not present them as market-typical figures in any student-facing revision.

### Fuel and Operating-Cost Data
Fuel prices used throughout (e.g., $3.50, $3.65, $3.75, $3.80, $3.90, $4.00/gallon) are fictional instructional assumptions, not current market data. Before any publication pass, confirm the module does not imply these are current — the EIA is the appropriate current-data source (see Primary Source Control below), and ATRI is appropriate only as an industry-research reference, never as an individual carrier's break-even substitute.

### Regulatory Boundaries
49 CFR § 371.3 is accurately described as governing broker recordkeeping and the shipper/receiving-party review right under § 371.3(c) — do not broaden this into a claim that every commercial document or shipper-broker contract must be disclosed. 49 CFR § 376.12 is not cited as governing authority for independent dispatcher-service agreements anywhere in this module; if referenced in a future revision, it must be clearly scoped to qualifying lease relationships between authorized carriers and equipment owners only.

### Terminology Discipline
Revenue, cash flow, contribution margin, and profit are kept distinct throughout. No calculation in this module should be labeled "profit" or "net profit" at any point — every dollar figure produced by this module's formulas is explicitly scoped (e.g., "estimated contribution margin," "revenue after fuel only").

## Primary Source Control

- eCFR, 49 CFR Part 371 / § 371.3 — https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-371 — broker recordkeeping and review-right baseline.
- U.S. Energy Information Administration, Diesel Fuel Prices — https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/gasdiesel/ — current on-highway diesel price data; verify before any publication pass, and never hard-code a specific price into evergreen instructional rules.
- American Transportation Research Institute — https://truckingresearch.org/ — industry-level operating-cost research; general context only, not a substitute for carrier-specific figures.
