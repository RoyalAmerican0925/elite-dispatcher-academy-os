## Answer Key

### Part A — Federal Weight and Size Figures
1. **b** — 80,000 lb gross baseline.
2. **b** — 20,000 lb single-axle limit.
3. **c** — 34,000 lb tandem-axle limit.
4. **c** — 102-inch National Network width.
5. **c** — 48-foot federal floor for minimum semitrailer length.

### Part B — Equipment Types and Concepts
6. **b** — Day cab has no sleeper berth; HOS regulates hours, not cab type.
7. **b** — Power-only: carrier supplies tractor only, trailer from another party.
8. **b** — Conestoga retraction configurations vary; verify the specific trailer.
9. **b** — GVWR is a specific manufacturer rating concept, not a casual figure.

### Part C — Equipment-Fit Principle
10. **c** — Multiple factors (weight, dimensions, axle distribution, loading method, equipment configuration, legal/route factors) may all matter; no single fact confirms fit.
11. **b** — Reefer trailers generally have less usable interior space than similarly sized dry vans due to insulation.
12. **b** — Fitting on a flatbed deck alone says nothing about legal height, securement, or route feasibility.

### Part D — True / False
13. **True** — Gross weight under 80,000 lb does not guarantee legal axle distribution; an individual axle or tandem group can exceed its own federal limit independently of the gross total.
14. **False** — There is no single nationwide federal maximum height limit applied uniformly; height limits are primarily state-controlled, with variation across jurisdictions.
15. **False** — Federal Hours of Service regulates hours of driving/on-duty time, not a numerical mileage cutoff.

### Part E — Equipment-Fit Classification
16. **BAD FIT** — 81,500 lb gross exceeds the 80,000 lb federal Interstate baseline with no permit indicated; this is not legal as stated.
17. **NEEDS MORE INFORMATION** — Conestoga retraction configuration must be verified before this can be classified as good or bad fit for overhead crane loading.
18. **GOOD FIT** (as to width) — 101 inches is within the 102-inch legal width allowance with no other dimensional conflicts noted; instructor note: remind students this addresses width only, not a full fit clearance.
19. **BAD FIT** — the driver lacks the required H or X endorsement for a placarded hazmat load; this is a hard qualification mismatch, not a resolvable equipment question.

### Item 20 — CRITICAL-FAILURE ITEM
20. **b** — BAD FIT. 35,100 lb on the drive tandem exceeds the federal 34,000 lb tandem-axle limit, independently illegal regardless of the 79,200 lb gross total being under the 80,000 lb baseline. **A student who selects (a) or (d) has failed this exam regardless of overall percentage score**, per the exam blueprint's critical-failure designation — this misconception ("under 80,000 lb gross always means legal") is the exact real-world error this module exists to prevent.

### Part F — Sample Reasoning
21. A combination's gross weight is the total of all axle weights combined, but each individual axle or axle group also has its own separate federal limit (20,000 lb single / 34,000 lb tandem). A combination can be under the 80,000 lb gross baseline overall while still having one axle group — most commonly the drive tandem — carrying more than its individual legal limit. Both checks (gross weight AND axle-group distribution) must pass independently; passing one does not guarantee the other.

22. This reasoning conflates "the trailer/equipment is rated to handle this weight" (a manufacturer/equipment capability question) with "this load is legally distributed across the axles for road travel" (a separate federal compliance question). Equipment being physically capable of carrying a weight says nothing about whether that weight is legally distributed across the vehicle's axle groups. A student should verify axle distribution independently rather than treating equipment rating as sufficient proof of legality.
