## B. Employment Outcome and Competency Map

**Observable occupational outcome:** the student can compare two or more loads properly — calculating effective RPM (not loaded RPM alone), estimating fuel cost from actual MPG and current fuel price, identifying applicable fees, and calculating contribution margin with explicitly stated included costs — rather than judging a load by its advertised rate alone.

**Competency IDs:**
- **calculate** loaded RPM and effective RPM, and explain why they can differ significantly
- **identify** the "loaded mile blindness" trap in a worked example
- **calculate** estimated fuel cost from actual truck MPG and a current fuel price (never a memorized figure)
- **state explicitly** which costs are included in any contribution-margin calculation
- **distinguish** contribution margin, cash flow, revenue, and accounting profit as four genuinely different figures
- **apply** the full load-comparison methodology (not loaded RPM alone) to select between competing loads
- **weigh** next-load positioning as a qualitative factor alongside the dollar calculation

## D. Lesson-by-Lesson Teaching Notes

**Loaded RPM vs. Effective RPM:**
- *Essential point:* loaded RPM (revenue ÷ loaded miles only) is the number most commonly advertised; effective RPM (revenue ÷ total trip miles, including deadhead) is what the load actually pays per mile driven.
- *Misconception to watch for (the single most important correction in this module):* students anchor on loaded RPM because it's the number they see first on a posting. Every example and drill should force a full effective-RPM calculation before any comparison judgment is allowed.

**The Deadhead Trap (core worked example):**
- *Purpose:* make loaded mile blindness concrete and memorable, not abstract.
- *Demonstration:* present two loads — one with a higher loaded RPM but heavy deadhead, one with a lower advertised rate but minimal deadhead — and have students calculate effective RPM for both before revealing which one actually pays more per total mile.
- *Debrief question:* "Which load would you have picked from the posting alone, and what changed once you calculated effective RPM?"

**Contribution margin discipline:**
- *Essential point:* contribution margin is revenue minus a specific, stated set of included costs — never presented without naming exactly what was subtracted.
- *Misconception to watch for:* students (and real-world beginners) treat contribution margin as if it were full accounting profit. It excludes driver pay, maintenance, insurance, equipment payments, permits, taxes, and overhead unless separately calculated. State this exclusion list explicitly every time the figure is used.
- *Workplace application:* when presenting a contribution-margin figure to a carrier, the dispatcher must state which costs were included — this is both a math discipline and a professional-communication discipline.

**Fuel cost estimation:**
- *Essential point:* total trip miles (not loaded miles) ÷ truck MPG × current fuel price per gallon. All three inputs must be the specific, actual, or current figures — not assumed averages.
- *Misconception to watch for:* students use loaded miles instead of total trip miles in the fuel calculation, understating the real cost.

**Load-comparison methodology:**
- *Essential point:* the full methodology — total trip miles, effective RPM, fuel cost from actual MPG and current price, applicable fees, contribution margin with stated included costs, and next-load positioning as a separate qualitative factor — must be applied as a sequence, not cherry-picked.
- *Misconception to watch for:* students skip straight to comparing loaded RPM figures, exactly the loaded-mile-blindness error the whole module exists to correct.

## E. Professional-Language Coaching

| Correct term | Common misuse | Consequence | Acceptable phrasing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effective RPM | "The rate" (ambiguous, usually means loaded RPM) | Compares loads on an incomplete basis, can lead to genuinely worse load selection | "Effective RPM on this one is $X, accounting for the deadhead — that's the number that actually matters for comparison." |
| Contribution margin | "Profit" | Overstates what the carrier will actually net after full costs | "Contribution margin here is $X, based on fuel and tolls only — not full profit." |
| Estimated fuel cost | A flat, memorized per-mile fuel figure | Produces a stale, potentially significantly wrong cost estimate | "Fuel cost estimate uses this truck's actual MPG and today's fuel price." |

**Language to correct on sight:** any student or dispatcher statement that treats loaded RPM alone as sufficient to judge a load, or that uses "contribution margin" and "profit" interchangeably.

## F. Activity Facilitation

**Load comparison worked exercises:**
- *Purpose:* force the full methodology under time pressure similar to a real dispatch desk.
- *Predictable error:* students revert to loaded-RPM-only comparison when rushed — this regression under pressure is itself useful diagnostic information, not just a wrong answer.
- *Debrief question:* "If you'd only had 30 seconds, what number would you have looked at first — and was that the right one?"
