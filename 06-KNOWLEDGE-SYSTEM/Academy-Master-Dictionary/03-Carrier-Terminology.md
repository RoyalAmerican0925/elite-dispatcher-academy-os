## Section 1: Carrier Types

### Motor Carrier
*(Already a controlled term — see `TERMINOLOGY-LEDGER.md`.)* The company that holds operating authority and physically transports freight, as distinct from a broker (which arranges transportation without transporting it).

### For-Hire Carrier
**What it means:** A motor carrier that transports property belonging to other companies, in exchange for compensation.
**What it does NOT mean:** The same thing as a private carrier (see below) — for-hire carriers move other parties' freight commercially; this is the type of carrier this academy's curriculum is built around.
**Common misuse:** Assuming every commercial truck on the road is automatically for-hire; some are private carriers moving their own company's goods.
**Operational example:** "This is a for-hire carrier operating for multiple different shipper customers."

### Private Carrier
**What it means:** A carrier that transports its own property as part of its own underlying business, rather than transporting other parties' freight for compensation.
**What it does NOT mean:** A carrier automatically exempt from all federal oversight — whether specific requirements apply depends on factors like vehicle weight and operation type, not simply private-vs-for-hire status (see Module 01's private-carrier caution).
**Common misuse:** Assuming private carriers face no regulatory requirements at all, when in fact many requirements can still apply depending on the specific operation.
**Operational example:** A retail company running its own trucks between its distribution centers and stores, moving only its own merchandise.

### Common Carrier vs. Contract Carrier
**What it means:** A common carrier generally holds itself out to serve the general public on a non-discriminatory basis; a contract carrier operates under specific, individually negotiated agreements with particular shippers rather than general public availability.
**What it does NOT mean:** A distinction that changes a carrier's basic for-hire status — both are types of for-hire carriage, differing in how they structure their customer relationships.
**Common misuse:** Assuming this distinction affects day-to-day dispatch decisions; for most carrier-side dispatching purposes, the practical workflow is similar regardless of this classification.
**Operational example:** "This carrier operates primarily under contract arrangements with a small number of regular shipper customers."

### Asset-Based Carrier vs. Non-Asset Carrier
**What it means:** An asset-based carrier owns the trucks/trailers it operates; a non-asset carrier arranges transportation without owning the underlying equipment.
**What it does NOT mean:** That "non-asset" is simply another word for broker — some non-asset operations still hold themselves out and operate differently than a broker arranging for others' authority; the specific business model and authority held control the actual classification.
**Common misuse:** Assuming every non-asset operation is automatically a broker, or that every asset-based company is automatically a for-hire carrier without checking.
**Operational example:** "This is an asset-based carrier — it owns the dry van fleet it dispatches."

## Section 2: Carrier Structure & Staffing

### Owner-Operator
**What it means:** An individual who owns their own truck and operates as an independent business, often contracting with a carrier to haul under that carrier's operating authority.
**What it does NOT mean:** The same thing as a company driver (see below) — an owner-operator owns the equipment and bears different business responsibilities and risks than an employee driver.
**Common misuse:** Assuming owner-operator status by itself resolves every classification question (tax, insurance, liability) — the actual relationship and agreement terms matter, not just the label.
**Operational example:** "The owner-operator leases their truck's operating authority through this carrier while running under a contractor agreement."

### Company Driver
**What it means:** A driver who is an employee of the motor carrier, typically driving carrier-owned or carrier-leased equipment.
**What it does NOT mean:** A universally identical employment structure — specific pay, benefits, and terms vary by carrier.
**Common misuse:** Assuming all drivers at a carrier share the same employment classification; a single carrier may use both company drivers and contracted owner-operators.
**Operational example:** "This driver is a company employee, operating a carrier-owned tractor."

### Lease Operator / Lease-On
**What it means:** An owner-operator who has formally leased their truck's operating authority to a specific carrier, allowing them to run under that carrier's authority and branding.
**What it does NOT mean:** Ownership transfer of the truck itself — lease-on arrangements typically concern operating authority and business relationship, not the underlying equipment ownership.
**Common misuse:** Confusing an operating-authority lease-on with an equipment lease/rental; they are different kinds of agreements that can exist independently of each other.
**Operational example:** "The owner-operator leased on with this carrier last month and now runs exclusively under its authority."

### Fleet
**What it means:** The collection of trucks/trailers a carrier operates as a group.
**What it does NOT mean:** A specific size threshold — "fleet" applies to operations ranging from a handful of trucks to thousands; there's no fixed number that defines when a fleet becomes a fleet.
**Common misuse:** Assuming "fleet" implies a large operation; a single-truck owner-operator's business is not usually called a fleet, but a modest multi-truck carrier already qualifies.
**Operational example:** "This carrier's fleet consists of twelve dry van units."

### Small Fleet / Mega Fleet
**What it means:** Informal industry size categories — small fleet generally refers to a handful to a few dozen trucks; mega fleet refers to very large carriers operating thousands of trucks.
**What it does NOT mean:** Precisely defined, universally agreed thresholds — these are informal industry descriptions, not regulatory categories with fixed cutoff numbers.
**Common misuse:** Treating "small fleet" or "mega fleet" as if they were official classifications with legal significance, rather than casual industry sizing language.
**Operational example:** "This is a small fleet of eight trucks, family-owned and operated."

### Carrier of Record
**What it means:** The specific motor carrier legally identified as responsible for a shipment under its own operating authority, regardless of which individual (owner-operator, company driver) physically operates the truck.
**What it does NOT mean:** The same thing as the truck's owner in every case — a leased-on owner-operator's load may show a different carrier of record than the individual's own separate business entity.
**Common misuse:** Assuming the name on the truck's door always matches the legal carrier of record without verifying.
**Operational example:** "Confirm the carrier of record on the rate confirmation matches the operating authority actually being used for this load."

### New Authority / "Green" Authority
**What it means:** Informal industry terms for a carrier that has recently obtained its operating authority, typically within roughly the first year.
**What it does NOT mean:** A formally defined regulatory status with one universal legal effect — "green authority" is industry shorthand; any specific consequences (e.g., some brokers' internal risk policies) depend on that broker or shipper's own rules, not a single federal standard.
**Common misuse:** Assuming "new authority" automatically means higher risk in every respect, without evaluating the carrier on its actual verified facts.
**Operational example:** "Some brokers apply extra scrutiny to new-authority carriers as part of their own internal risk policy."

## Section 3: Authority & Registration (Introductory Only)

**Note:** Full regulatory depth on USDOT numbers, MC/operating authority, safety ratings, and registration procedure belongs to the future **Compliance/Regulatory terminology** branch and to Modules 01, 04, and 06, which already carry sourced, verified treatment of this material. This section only introduces the vocabulary at a recognition level.

### Operating Authority
*(Introduced in Module 01, expanded in Module 04.)* The separate federal registration that authorizes a company to conduct specified regulated transportation operations, distinct from a USDOT number, which identifies the entity.

### USDOT Number
*(Introduced in Module 01.)* An identifier used by FMCSA to identify and track a regulated entity — identifies who the company is, not what it's authorized to do.

### MC Number
**What it means:** A specific type of operating-authority docket number historically associated with for-hire property carrier authority (Motor Carrier number).
**What it does NOT mean:** The same thing as a USDOT number — see Module 01's USDOT-vs-authority distinction, which this term inherits directly.
**Common misuse:** Using "MC number" and "USDOT number" interchangeably in professional communication; they identify different things.
**Operational example:** "Verify both the USDOT number and the MC operating authority before booking with an unfamiliar carrier."

### Carrier Packet
**What it means:** The standard set of documents (authority information, insurance information, W-9, references, etc.) a carrier provides to a broker or shipper when establishing a new working relationship.
**What it does NOT mean:** A substitute for independent verification — a carrier packet contains represented information that still benefits from independent confirmation where it matters (see Module 04's COI caution as one example of this principle).
**Common misuse:** Treating everything in a carrier packet as automatically current and verified without any independent check.
**Operational example:** "The broker requested a current carrier packet before setting up the new lane."

### Trailer Interchange Agreement
**What it means:** An agreement allowing one carrier's trailer to be used or exchanged with another carrier, common in intermodal and drop-and-hook operations.
**What it does NOT mean:** A transfer of ownership — interchange agreements govern usage and liability terms for equipment that remains owned by its original party.
**Common misuse:** Assuming any trailer swap between carriers is automatically covered without an actual interchange agreement in place.
**Operational example:** "Confirm a trailer interchange agreement exists before dispatching a driver to swap trailers with another carrier."

## Section 4: Insurance & Financial Basics (Introductory Only)

**Note:** Full depth on insurance types, coverage minimums, and financial-responsibility mechanics belongs to Modules 04 and 06, and to the future **Compliance/Regulatory** and **Billing/Factoring** branches. This section only introduces the vocabulary.

### Cargo Insurance
**What it means:** Insurance coverage protecting against loss or damage to the freight being transported.
**What it does NOT mean:** A federally mandated coverage for all for-hire carriers in every case — cargo insurance is commonly required contractually by brokers/shippers, distinct from the federally required public-liability (BIPD) coverage (see Module 04, Section 4).
**Common misuse:** Assuming cargo insurance and public-liability insurance are the same coverage; they protect against different things.
**Operational example:** "Confirm the carrier's cargo insurance covers the declared value of this specific commodity."

### Certificate of Insurance (COI)
*(Introduced in Module 04.)* A document summarizing represented insurance information — useful, but not proof by itself of current operating authority or that coverage hasn't changed since issuance.

### Non-Trucking Liability / Bobtail Insurance
**What it means:** Insurance coverage for a truck when it's being operated for personal use or bobtailing (no trailer, not under dispatch for a load) — distinct from the coverage that applies while actively hauling a load under the carrier's operating authority.
**What it does NOT mean:** Coverage for cargo or for the truck while actively dispatched on a load — non-trucking liability specifically fills the gap outside active dispatch operation.
**Common misuse:** Assuming a single insurance policy automatically covers every situation a truck might be in, regardless of whether it's actively dispatched.
**Operational example:** "The owner-operator's non-trucking liability policy covers the truck when it's not under active dispatch."

## Section 5: Carrier-Dispatcher Relationship

### Represented Carrier
*(Central to Module 02's carrier-side agency model.)* The specific motor carrier a dispatcher has an ongoing, preexisting agreement with and coordinates loads for — the foundation of the carrier-side dispatch relationship this academy teaches.

### Dispatch Agreement
**What it means:** The written agreement between a dispatch business and a carrier establishing the terms of their working relationship — scope, compensation, responsibilities, and authority.
**What it does NOT mean:** A document whose title alone determines legal classification — actual conduct controls over labels (see Module 02's label-vs-conduct discipline).
**Common misuse:** Treating a dispatch agreement's existence as automatically resolving every question about the relationship's legal classification.
**Operational example:** "Review the dispatch agreement's compensation and scope terms before taking on a new represented carrier."

### LPOA (Limited Power of Attorney)
*(Introduced in Module 02.)* A document granting specifically defined agency authority according to its own written terms — scope-limited, not a blanket signing authority for every purpose.

### Carrier Onboarding
*(Central to Module 04.)* The process of establishing a new represented-carrier relationship — verifying identity, authority, insurance, and building the driver/equipment profiles needed to safely dispatch.

### Client Carrier / Represented Carrier Roster
**What it means:** The set of carriers a dispatch operation actively represents and coordinates loads for.
**What it does NOT mean:** An unlimited or automatically safe number — as Module 02 teaches, representing multiple carriers is not itself risky, but exercising discretion to allocate a single freight opportunity among them is (see Module 02's allocation-of-traffic discipline).
**Common misuse:** Assuming roster size alone determines legal risk, rather than the actual conduct involved in how loads are sourced and assigned across that roster.
**Operational example:** "The represented-carrier roster includes three carriers, each operating in genuinely different equipment/territory scopes."

## Section 6: Carrier Selection & Relationship Management

### Preferred Carrier / Approved Carrier List
**What it means:** A broker's or shipper's own internal list of carriers they choose to work with regularly, typically based on that party's own vetting criteria.
**What it does NOT mean:** A federal or industry-wide certification — "preferred" or "approved" status is specific to the broker/shipper maintaining the list, not a universal credential.
**Common misuse:** Assuming preferred-carrier status with one broker automatically carries any weight with a different, unrelated broker or shipper.
**Operational example:** "This carrier is on the broker's approved list after completing their onboarding review."

### Core Carrier
**What it means:** A carrier that a shipper or broker relies on as a primary, go-to resource for a given lane or type of freight, typically due to an established track record.
**What it does NOT mean:** An exclusive or guaranteed arrangement — core-carrier status typically means priority consideration, not a binding commitment to only use that carrier.
**Common misuse:** Assuming core-carrier status guarantees a fixed volume of freight without an actual contractual commitment to that effect.
**Operational example:** "We've become a core carrier for this shipper's Midwest lanes over the past year."

### Backup Carrier
**What it means:** A carrier kept in reserve to cover freight when a primary or core carrier can't take a load.
**What it does NOT mean:** A lesser or unqualified carrier — backup status reflects a broker/shipper's operational sequencing, not necessarily carrier quality.
**Common misuse:** Assuming backup-carrier status is a negative reflection on the carrier rather than simply a role in that specific broker/shipper's routing plan.
**Operational example:** "We're the backup carrier on this lane — available if the primary carrier can't cover it."

### Carrier Vetting / Carrier Selection
**What it means:** The process a broker, shipper, or dispatch operation uses to evaluate whether a carrier is a good fit for a working relationship — checking authority, insurance, safety history, and operational fit.
**What it does NOT mean:** A one-time check that never needs updating — vetting is most useful when treated as an ongoing practice, not a single pass at initial onboarding.
**Common misuse:** Treating initial vetting as permanently sufficient without any periodic re-confirmation of authority, insurance, or standing.
**Operational example:** "Carrier vetting confirmed active authority, current insurance, and no major red flags before adding them to the roster."

### Carrier Scorecard
**What it means:** An internal tracking tool some brokers/shippers/carriers use to record a carrier's or driver's performance over time — on-time percentage, communication quality, claims history, and similar factors.
**What it does NOT mean:** A standardized, universal, or federally mandated score — scorecards are internal tools, and their specific criteria and weighting vary by whoever maintains them.
**Common misuse:** Assuming a scorecard rating from one company carries the same meaning or weight with a different company's own internal scorecard.
**Operational example:** "This carrier's internal scorecard shows strong on-time performance over the last quarter."
