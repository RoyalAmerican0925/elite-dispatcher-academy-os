## Section 1: Trailer Types

### Dry Van
**What it means:** A fully enclosed, non-temperature-controlled trailer, the most common general-purpose equipment type.
**What it does NOT mean:** Equipment with any temperature-control capability — a dry van cannot substitute for a reefer regardless of how the load is described (see Module 03's equipment-fit discipline).
**Common misuse:** Assuming a dry van can handle any commodity requiring temperature control "just this once."
**Operational example:** "Standard dry goods, no temperature control needed — a dry van is appropriate here."

### Reefer (Refrigerated Trailer)
**What it means:** A trailer equipped with an active temperature-control unit, used for temperature-sensitive freight.
**What it does NOT mean:** The same usable interior space as a similarly sized dry van — reefer insulation reduces usable interior space and reefer units are typically heavier, reducing available cargo weight too (see Module 03).
**Common misuse:** Assuming a reefer and dry van of the same nominal length offer identical capacity.
**Operational example:** "This load requires continuous -10°F — confirm the reefer unit's actual operating range and fuel status before dispatch."

### Flatbed
**What it means:** An open-deck trailer with no sides or roof, used for freight that can be loaded from the top or sides, or that doesn't fit inside an enclosed trailer.
**What it does NOT mean:** Equipment where "fits on the deck" alone confirms a load can legally move — fitting on the deck says nothing about legal height, required securement, weight distribution, or route feasibility (see Module 03, Myth 5).
**Common misuse:** Assuming flatbed freight requires no securement analysis simply because it physically sits on the deck.
**Operational example:** "The machinery fits on the flatbed deck, but securement, height, and route clearance all still need to be confirmed separately."

### Step Deck (Drop Deck)
**What it means:** A flatbed-style trailer with two deck levels — a higher front deck and a lower main deck — allowing taller freight to be hauled while staying within legal height limits.
**What it does NOT mean:** Unlimited height capacity — a step deck lowers the deck height to buy additional freight-height allowance, but legal height limits still apply to the total loaded height (see Module 03's height-limit content).
**Common misuse:** Assuming a step deck eliminates height concerns entirely rather than simply providing additional clearance.
**Operational example:** "The step deck's lower main deck allows this tall equipment to stay within the state's legal height limit."

### Lowboy / Double-Drop Trailer
**What it means:** A specialized flatbed-style trailer with an even lower deck height than a step deck, typically used for very tall or heavy equipment such as construction machinery.
**What it does NOT mean:** A trailer suited for standard general freight — lowboys are specialized equipment for specific oversize/heavy-equipment applications.
**Common misuse:** Assuming any oversize load can move on a standard flatbed rather than requiring genuinely specialized equipment like a lowboy.
**Operational example:** "This heavy construction equipment requires a lowboy, not a standard flatbed, given its height and weight."

### Conestoga
**What it means:** A trailer with a retractable, tarp-like curtain system covering a flatbed-style deck, combining some flatbed loading flexibility with weather protection.
**What it does NOT mean:** A guarantee of overhead crane-loading capability — Conestoga retraction configurations vary by specific trailer, and whether a given unit retracts far enough for overhead loading must be verified for that specific trailer (see Module 03, Section 11).
**Common misuse:** Assuming all Conestoga trailers can be crane-loaded from above without confirming the specific unit's configuration.
**Operational example:** "Verify this specific Conestoga's retraction configuration before committing to overhead crane loading."

### Power-Only
**What it means:** An arrangement where the carrier supplies only the tractor (power unit); the trailer comes from another party.
**What it does NOT mean:** That the carrier supplies the trailer — this is a common beginner mix-up (see Module 03, Myth 8).
**Common misuse:** Assuming trailer availability exists under a power-only arrangement without confirming where the trailer is actually coming from.
**Operational example:** "This is a power-only move — confirm which party is providing the trailer before dispatch."

### Intermodal Chassis
**What it means:** A wheeled frame designed to carry a shipping container over the road, used specifically in drayage and intermodal moves.
**What it does NOT mean:** A standard over-the-road trailer — a chassis is built specifically to carry containers, not general freight directly.
**Common misuse:** Confusing chassis availability/condition issues with standard trailer availability issues; chassis pools and interchange agreements are a distinct intermodal-specific concern.
**Operational example:** "Confirm chassis availability at the rail ramp before committing to this drayage move."

### Tanker / Tank Trailer
**What it means:** A trailer designed to carry liquids or gases in bulk, requiring specialized equipment and (per Trucking Terminology's N endorsement entry) a specific driver qualification.
**What it does NOT mean:** Equipment appropriate for non-bulk liquid freight, like palletized bottled beverages — non-bulk liquid in standard packaging does not require tank-vehicle equipment or the N endorsement (see Module 03's scenario distinguishing bulk from non-bulk liquid freight).
**Common misuse:** Assuming any liquid commodity automatically requires tanker equipment and an N/X endorsement, without checking whether the freight is actually bulk or simply packaged liquid.
**Operational example:** "Palletized bottled water in a standard dry van does not require tanker equipment or a tank-vehicle endorsement — confirm the commodity is genuinely bulk before assuming otherwise."

## Section 2: Weight & Dimension (Cross-Referencing Module 03)

### Federal Gross Weight Baseline
*(Established in Module 03, FACT-M03-001.)* The 80,000 lb combined gross weight baseline on the Interstate System, applying to the entire loaded vehicle combination — tractor, trailer, and cargo together, not cargo alone.

### Federal Axle Weight Limits
*(Established in Module 03, FACT-M03-002.)* The federal single-axle (20,000 lb) and tandem-axle (34,000 lb) limits on the Interstate System — independent checks from the gross weight baseline. A combination can be under 80,000 lb gross and still have an illegal individual axle group.

### National Network Width
*(Established in Module 03, FACT-M03-003.)* The standard 102-inch width figure federal rules generally require states to permit on the National Network — exceeding it does not automatically mean a load is illegal, and does not automatically mean a permit is guaranteed either; both require verification (see Module 03, Myth 12).

### Federal Height Limit (Absence Of)
*(Established in Module 03, FACT-M03-004.)* There is no comparable federal maximum height limit — height limits are primarily state-controlled, and commonly cited figures are state examples, not a federal rule.

### Nominal Size vs. Actual Usable Dimensions
*(Central to Module 03's teaching, e.g., Myth 3.)* A trailer's advertised nominal size ("53-foot") is a starting point, not a guarantee — actual usable interior length, width, height, and door opening size vary by manufacturer and by trailer condition/configuration.

### Tare Weight
**What it means:** The actual weight of an empty tractor-trailer combination, before any cargo is loaded.
**What it does NOT mean:** A fixed, universal figure for all units of the same nominal type — tare weight varies by specific unit, and available cargo-weight margin must be calculated from the actual current tare weight of the specific combination, not an assumed average (see Module 07's weight-margin relationship).
**Common misuse:** Assuming a standard tare weight for "any 53-foot dry van" rather than confirming the specific unit's actual current weight.
**Operational example:** "Confirm this specific tractor-trailer's actual tare weight before calculating available cargo-weight margin for this load."

### Available Gross-Weight Margin
*(Established in Module 07, Section 27.)* Applicable gross limit minus actual operating weight before cargo — a useful starting figure, but not the same as "legal payload" without further qualification, since axle limits, Bridge Formula requirements, and other factors can still reduce actual legal loadability.

### Federal Bridge Formula
**What it means:** A federal formula relating a vehicle combination's axle spacing to its permissible weight, used to prevent excessive weight concentration over short spans of roadway/bridges.
**What it does NOT mean:** The same thing as the simple single-axle or tandem-axle weight limits — the Bridge Formula is an additional, separate calculation based on axle spacing, not a replacement for checking individual axle-group limits.
**Common misuse:** Assuming a load that passes the basic gross-weight and tandem-axle checks automatically satisfies Bridge Formula requirements as well, without a separate check.
**Operational example:** "This combination's axle spacing needs a Bridge Formula check in addition to the standard gross and tandem-weight checks."

### Overwidth / Oversize
*(Established in Module 03, Scenario S-03-13.)* Freight exceeding the standard 102-inch width figure (overwidth) or otherwise exceeding standard legal dimensions (oversize) — not automatically illegal, and not automatically permittable either; requires verification of the specific states, routes, permit process, and equipment involved.

### Permit Load
**What it means:** A load requiring a specific state or route permit due to exceeding standard legal weight or dimension limits.
**What it does NOT mean:** A load that can be assumed permittable without verification — permit availability, process, and lead time are state- and route-specific and must be confirmed, not assumed (see Module 03, Section 21).
**Common misuse:** Booking an overweight or oversize load before confirming the specific permit process and lead time for the actual states and routes involved.
**Operational example:** "This is a permit load — confirm the permit process and lead time for the specific states involved before committing to a delivery date."

### Escort Vehicle / Pilot Car
**What it means:** A separate vehicle that accompanies an oversize load to help manage traffic, warn other drivers, or navigate tight clearances, often required by state permit conditions for sufficiently large loads.
**What it does NOT mean:** A requirement for every oversize load — whether an escort vehicle is required depends on the specific load's dimensions and the specific state/route's requirements.
**Common misuse:** Assuming escort-vehicle requirements (or their absence) without checking the specific permit conditions for the actual load and route.
**Operational example:** "Confirm whether the state's permit conditions for this load require an escort vehicle before dispatch."

## Section 3: Driver/Equipment Qualification Cross-Reference

### H Endorsement
*(Established in Module 03, Section 25.)* A CDL endorsement applying in specified regulated circumstances involving hazardous materials — not a universal requirement for every load described as "hazmat."

### N Endorsement
*(Established in Module 03, Section 25.)* A CDL endorsement applying to operating a tank vehicle as defined under applicable federal rules — about the vehicle configuration (a tank), not about the material being hazardous.

### X Endorsement
*(Established in Module 03, Section 25.)* The combined tank-vehicle-plus-hazardous-materials qualification — required when a driver operates a tank vehicle that is also carrying hazardous materials requiring placarding. A driver holding only H does not satisfy a load requiring X.

### Placarding
**What it means:** The display of required warning signage on a vehicle carrying hazardous materials, with specific requirements depending on the material and quantity involved.
**What it does NOT mean:** A single universal requirement triggered by any hazardous material in any quantity — placarding requirements are material- and quantity-specific (see Module 03, Section 25).
**Common misuse:** Assuming placarding is or isn't required without checking the specific material and quantity's actual requirements.
**Operational example:** "Verify the specific placarding requirement for this material and quantity rather than assuming based on the general commodity type."

## Section 4: Equipment Condition & Maintenance

### Pre-Trip / Post-Trip Inspection
*(Already covered in `01-Trucking-Terminology.md`.)* A driver's required check of the vehicle's condition before beginning a trip and after completing one, covering safety-critical components.

### Out of Service (OOS)
**What it means:** A designation indicating a vehicle or driver has been determined unfit to continue operating until a specific deficiency is corrected.
**What it does NOT mean:** A permanent condition — an out-of-service designation is typically correctable, after which the vehicle or driver may resume operation.
**Common misuse:** Treating an out-of-service designation as an automatic, permanent disqualification rather than a correctable condition requiring specific action.
**Operational example:** "The unit was placed out of service for a specific defect — confirm the correction and clearance before redispatching."

### Preventive Maintenance (PM)
**What it means:** Scheduled maintenance performed on a regular basis to keep equipment in safe operating condition, rather than only responding to breakdowns as they occur.
**What it does NOT mean:** A substitute for a pre-trip inspection — scheduled PM and the driver's own pre-trip inspection serve different, complementary purposes.
**Common misuse:** Assuming a unit is safe to dispatch simply because it's within its PM schedule, without the driver's own pre-trip inspection.
**Operational example:** "The unit is current on its PM schedule, but the driver's pre-trip inspection is still required before this specific dispatch."

### Breakdown
**What it means:** A mechanical failure that prevents a vehicle from continuing to operate as planned.
**What it does NOT mean:** An event with a single standard resolution process — the appropriate response depends on the specific failure, location, and load requirements (full exception-handling procedure reserved for the future Exception/Problem-Resolution terminology branch).
**Common misuse:** Assuming every breakdown follows the same resolution timeline or process regardless of the specific mechanical issue and circumstances.
**Operational example:** "Document the breakdown location, cause, and estimated repair time before communicating an updated status to the broker."
