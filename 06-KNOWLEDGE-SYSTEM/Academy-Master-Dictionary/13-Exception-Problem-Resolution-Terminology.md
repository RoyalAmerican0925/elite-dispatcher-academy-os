## Section 1: Core Exception-Handling Discipline

### Exception
**What it means:** Any deviation from a load's expected, normal course — a delay, a discrepancy, a refusal, a mechanical failure, or any other unplanned event requiring a decision.
**What it does NOT mean:** A crisis requiring panic or an immediate unilateral decision — the same STOP/VERIFY/ESCALATE discipline used throughout this academy applies to exceptions as much as to any other uncertain situation.
**Common misuse:** Treating every exception as equally serious, rather than assessing what kind of exception it actually is and what response it actually calls for.
**Operational example:** "This is an exception — pause, gather the actual facts, and determine whether it needs simple documentation or escalation."

### Exception Handling
**What it means:** The general process of recognizing, documenting, and appropriately responding to an exception as it arises.
**What it does NOT mean:** A single fixed procedure applicable identically to every kind of exception — a mechanical breakdown, a refused delivery, and a payment dispute all call for different specific steps, even though they share the same underlying STOP/VERIFY/ESCALATE discipline.
**Common misuse:** Applying a one-size-fits-all response to every exception type rather than recognizing what kind of problem is actually being handled.
**Operational example:** "Exception handling for a breakdown looks different from exception handling for a refused delivery — both start with the same discipline, but the specific next steps diverge from there."

### Documentation (Exception Context)
**What it means:** The practice of creating a clear, contemporaneous written record of an exception — what happened, when, and what was observed — as it's discovered or resolved.
**What it does NOT mean:** An optional step to skip when a situation feels informally resolved — documentation is what supports any later claim, dispute, or review, and its absence is a real practical cost even when a problem seems to have worked itself out.
**Common misuse:** Resolving a problem verbally without creating any written record, leaving no support if a question comes up later.
**Operational example:** "Document the exception now, while the facts are fresh, even if the immediate problem already seems resolved."

### Root Cause
**What it means:** The actual underlying reason an exception occurred, as distinct from its symptoms or immediate trigger.
**What it does NOT mean:** Something a dispatcher should assume or assign without actual investigation — attributing fault (to the carrier, the shipper, the receiver, or another party) requires verified facts, not a convenient guess.
**Common misuse:** Concluding a root cause from incomplete information rather than the facts actually gathered.
**Operational example:** "Document the observed facts first — root cause should be determined from evidence, not assumed to assign blame quickly."

## Section 2: Delivery & Freight-Condition Exceptions (Cross-Reference)

**Note:** These terms are already fully defined in `05-Shipper-Receiver-Terminology.md` and are cross-referenced, not restated, here.

### Refused Freight
*(Already established in Trucking Terminology and Shipper/Receiver Terminology.)* Freight a receiver declines to accept at delivery — typically requires escalation and documentation, not an independent on-site decision by the driver or dispatcher.

### Short / Shortage
*(Already established in Shipper/Receiver Terminology.)* Freight delivered in a quantity less than documented as shipped — not automatic proof of carrier fault; the cause requires investigation, not assumption.

### Overage
*(Already established in Shipper/Receiver Terminology.)* Freight delivered in a quantity greater than documented as shipped — should be documented and reported, not simply accepted without flagging the discrepancy.

### Concealed Damage
*(Already established in Shipper/Receiver Terminology.)* Damage discovered only after a shipment is opened, sometimes after the driver has departed — involves different timing and documentation considerations than damage noted at time of delivery.

## Section 3: Operational Exceptions (Cross-Reference)

### Breakdown
*(Already established in Equipment Terminology.)* A mechanical failure preventing a vehicle from continuing to operate as planned — the appropriate response depends on the specific failure, location, and load requirements, not a single fixed process.

### No-Show
*(Already established in Dispatch Terminology.)* A driver's failure to arrive for a scheduled appointment without prior notice — distinct from a documented, communicated delay.

### Load Cancellation
*(Already established in Dispatch Terminology.)* A formal decision by the broker, shipper, or carrier to call off a previously booked load — not a cost-free or purely administrative event; can carry financial and relationship consequences depending on timing and the applicable agreement.

### Bumped Load / Bumped Appointment
*(Already established in Trucking Terminology.)* A situation where a scheduled load or appointment is displaced or delayed, often due to facility-side congestion — not necessarily caused by carrier lateness, and not automatically entitled to detention pay without checking the applicable agreement.

## Section 4: Dispute & Claim Vocabulary

### Claim
**What it means:** A formal request for compensation related to a problem with a shipment — typically for damage, shortage, or a similar loss.
**What it does NOT mean:** A determination of fault or an admission of liability — filing or receiving a claim initiates a review process; it does not itself establish who is actually responsible.
**Common misuse:** Treating the filing of a claim as equivalent to an established finding of fault.
**Operational example:** "A claim has been filed for the reported shortage — that starts the review process; it doesn't itself establish what caused the discrepancy."

### Liability (General Concept)
**What it means:** Legal or financial responsibility for a loss or problem.
**What it does NOT mean:** Something a dispatcher is positioned to determine independently — this academy consistently declines to train dispatchers as legal adjudicators (the same principle that runs through Module 02's "a dispatcher is not positioned to make this legal determination" framing applies equally here).
**Common misuse:** A dispatcher declaring who is liable for a loss based on incomplete information, rather than documenting facts and escalating the determination appropriately.
**Operational example:** "Document what's known and escalate the liability question rather than declaring a conclusion the available facts don't yet support."

### Dispute Resolution
**What it means:** The general process of working through a disagreement between parties (carrier, broker, shipper, receiver) about a shipment-related problem, typically involving documentation review and negotiation.
**What it does NOT mean:** A process with one fixed procedure across every situation — the actual applicable process depends on the parties' agreements and the nature of the dispute.
**Common misuse:** Assuming a single standard dispute-resolution procedure applies universally without checking the actual applicable agreement.
**Operational example:** "Review the applicable agreement's dispute-resolution terms rather than assuming a generic process applies."

### Freight Claim Deadline
**What it means:** A time limit within which a claim must be filed to be considered, as established by the applicable agreement or governing terms.
**What it does NOT mean:** A universal, fixed number of days across every relationship — specific deadlines depend on the applicable agreement and are not something this academy asserts as a fixed figure.
**Common misuse:** Assuming a specific claim-filing deadline without confirming the actual applicable terms.
**Operational example:** "Confirm the applicable claim-filing deadline in the governing agreement rather than assuming a standard timeframe."

## Section 5: Escalation & Resolution Discipline

### STOP / VERIFY / ESCALATE (Exception Context)
*(Core methodology already established in Dispatch Terminology, applied here specifically to exceptions.)* Stop before drawing a conclusion about an exception's cause or resolution; verify the actual facts through an appropriate source; escalate if the situation exceeds ordinary dispatcher judgment or the facts remain unresolved after verification.

### Escalation Path (Exception Context)
*(Already established in Dispatch Terminology.)* The defined chain of who a dispatcher contacts when an exception exceeds their own authority or judgment — a normal, expected part of exception handling, not a sign of failure.

### Corrective Action
**What it means:** A specific step taken to resolve or prevent the recurrence of a problem identified through an exception.
**What it does NOT mean:** Automatically the same response for every exception — the appropriate corrective action depends on the specific root cause actually identified.
**Common misuse:** Applying a generic corrective action without connecting it to the actual, verified cause of the specific exception.
**Operational example:** "Once the root cause is confirmed, identify the specific corrective action that addresses it — not a generic response."

### Follow-Up
**What it means:** Continued communication and tracking after an exception's initial resolution, to confirm the issue is genuinely closed and hasn't recurred.
**What it does NOT mean:** An optional final step — an exception that appears resolved in the moment can still require follow-up to confirm it's actually fully closed out (documentation, payment, or corrective action all completed).
**Common misuse:** Considering an exception closed as soon as the immediate problem seems to have gone away, without confirming all follow-up steps (documentation, billing, corrective action) were actually completed.
**Operational example:** "Follow up in a few days to confirm the claim was actually processed, rather than assuming it's handled once submitted."
