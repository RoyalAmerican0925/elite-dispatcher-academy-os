# Academy Master Dictionary — Professional Communication Guide

**Status:** DRAFT_COMPLETE — QA required before PUBLICATION_READY
**Section:** Third top-level section of the Academy Master Dictionary framework, alongside the Master Dictionary and the Acronym Dictionary.
**Purpose:** How a carrier-side dispatcher actually communicates with each party they deal with — tone, structure, and professional norms across phone, email, written notes, and load-board contact. This is a communication-style guide, not a vocabulary list; it draws on the terminology already built across all 13 dictionary branches rather than redefining any of it.
**Governing principle, repeated throughout:** Accurately represent your actual role and the carrier you're working for, every time, with every audience. Never claim to be someone you're not — not the carrier owner, not the driver, not the broker, not another employee. This rule is absolute and appears in every section below.

---

## Section 1: Speaking With Carriers

If you dispatch as a separate service for a carrier (rather than as that carrier's own employee), your communication with the carrier owner/manager is its own professional relationship, distinct from how you speak with brokers, drivers, or facilities.

**Tone:** Direct, factual, and grounded in the carrier's own standing instructions and preferences (see Dispatch Terminology's Standing Instructions entry). You're bringing the carrier information and options — not making their business decisions for them.

**What to communicate, and how:**
- **Load options:** Present the numbers plainly — effective RPM, not just loaded RPM; contribution margin with included costs stated explicitly; next-load positioning as a separate factor (see Rate/Pricing Terminology). Let the carrier decide.
- **Problems:** Report facts first, then your assessment, then options. "The driver hit a bumped appointment at the receiver — no fault on our end, we're now working a new window. Wanted you aware in case it affects your next planning."
- **Recommendations:** Frame as recommendations, not decisions already made on the carrier's behalf, unless your actual dispatch agreement and standing instructions clearly authorize you to decide without checking first.

**What NOT to do:** Don't present a load as already booked when it's still pending carrier approval. Don't understate a problem to avoid an uncomfortable conversation — the carrier needs accurate information to make good decisions.

## Section 2: Speaking With Drivers

**Tone:** Clear, respectful, and operationally precise. Drivers need accurate, complete information delivered efficiently — not vague instructions that leave gaps for them to guess at.

**A professional dispatch instruction includes:**
- Exact pickup/delivery locations and appointment windows
- What's known and what's still being verified (never present a guess as a confirmed fact — see the Missing Information discipline in Dispatch Terminology)
- Any special handling, facility requirements, or equipment notes relevant to that specific load
- How to reach you if something changes

**Example:** *"Pickup at [shipper name], [address], appointment window 8–10 AM tomorrow. Dry van, dock loading, no special handling noted. Delivery details still being confirmed with the broker — I'll have that to you before you're empty. Call me if the appointment window becomes a problem."*

**What NOT to do:** Don't pressure a driver to log hours that don't reflect their actual duty status (see HOS Terminology's Duty Status entry — this is the driver's own accurate record, not something dispatch directs). Don't give a driver instructions based on an assumption when the actual fact is still unverified — tell them what's confirmed and what's pending.

### Check Calls
Check calls should be genuinely two-way — confirm status, ask about anything relevant (fuel, hours, any developing issues), and give the driver a chance to flag a concern before it becomes a bigger problem. A check call that's purely one-directional ("just confirming you're still moving") misses half its value.

## Section 3: Speaking With Brokers

*(Full script already established in Module 05, Section 33 — cross-referenced, not restated, here.)*

**Core model:** *"Hello, this is [Name], dispatching for [Carrier Legal Name], MC [number]. I'm calling about load [reference number] from [origin] to [destination]. Is it still available?"*

**Beyond the opening:** Once availability is confirmed, work through the missing-information categories systematically (see the Broker Call Sheet in `04-STUDENT-TOOLS/Module-05-Tools/`) rather than a scattered back-and-forth. Confirm details, get the rate discussion done clearly, and always follow up any verbal agreement with a request for the written rate confirmation — a verbal confirmation call supports but never replaces document review (see Documentation Terminology, §3).

**What NOT to do:** Never claim to be the carrier owner, the driver, or anyone other than an accurately identified dispatcher working on behalf of the named carrier. Never adopt a deceptive script to get information or pressure a broker — professional and accurate is the whole model here.

## Section 4: Speaking With Facilities (Shippers & Receivers)

**Tone:** Professional, brief, and focused on confirming operational specifics — facilities deal with many carriers and dispatchers; efficient, accurate communication is genuinely appreciated.

**Typical facility-side calls:**
- **Confirming an appointment:** "Calling to confirm the appointment window for load [reference number], pickup/delivery scheduled for [date/time]."
- **Requesting facility requirements in advance:** "Before I dispatch a driver, can you confirm your check-in procedure and any facility requirements — PPE, specific documentation, loading method?" (see Shipper/Receiver Terminology, §2–3)
- **Handling a delay or exception:** State the fact plainly, ask what's needed next. "Our driver is running about an hour behind the appointment window due to [reason] — what's the best way to handle that on your end?"

**What NOT to do:** Don't demand special treatment or imply entitlement to a specific outcome (like guaranteed detention pay) during a facility call — that's a documentation and billing question to resolve separately, not something to argue in the moment with dock staff.

## Section 5: Phone Etiquette

- State who you are and who you represent immediately — don't make the other party guess or ask.
- Have the load/reference number ready before calling; searching for it mid-call wastes both parties' time.
- If leaving a voicemail, state your name, carrier, callback number, and the specific reason for the call — clearly enough that the other party can act on it even if they can't call back immediately.
- Confirm anything important (a rate, an appointment time, an instruction) by repeating it back before ending the call.

## Section 6: Email Etiquette

- Use a clear, specific subject line including the load/reference number where applicable — not a vague subject like "Question" or "Load."
- State the purpose of the email in the first sentence; don't bury the actual ask or update several paragraphs in.
- When following up a phone conversation in writing (recommended for anything material — a rate, an instruction, a resolved discrepancy), summarize what was agreed so there's a written record.
- Treat any email requesting a change to payment or contact instructions with the same STOP/VERIFY discipline taught throughout this academy — verify through a previously trusted channel before acting on an emailed request to change sensitive information (see Broker Terminology's Payment Redirection entry).

## Section 7: Load-Board Contact Language

*(Core script already established in Module 05 and cross-referenced above in Section 3.)*

When reaching out about a posted load, lead with accurate identification and the specific load reference — the same discipline as any broker call, just initiated from a board listing rather than an inbound tender. Avoid vague opening messages ("Interested in this load") that don't identify who you are or what carrier you represent; specific, professional first contact gets faster, better responses.

## Section 8: Professional Written Notes

*(Dispatch Note already established in Dispatch Terminology, §4.)*

A good dispatch note is:
- **Factual** — what happened, when, who was involved — not editorializing or assigning blame before the facts are confirmed.
- **Specific enough to be useful later** — "driver authorized to wait for new appointment after being bumped" is useful; "handled the delay" is not.
- **Timely** — written close to when the event happened, not reconstructed from memory days later.

**Example of a weak note:** "Problem with delivery, fixed it."
**Example of a strong note:** "Receiver bumped the 10 AM appointment due to dock congestion; new window confirmed for 2 PM same day. Driver notified. No detention exposure since receiver initiated the delay before check-in."
