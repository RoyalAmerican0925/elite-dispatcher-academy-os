## Section 1: Core Revenue & Rate Concepts

### Gross Linehaul Revenue
*(Established in Module 07.)* The base line-haul payment for moving the freight, before accessorials.

### Total Gross Load Revenue
*(Established in Module 07.)* Gross linehaul revenue plus any applicable accessorial revenue actually earned on that load — not the same as gross linehaul revenue alone.

### Loaded Rate Per Mile (Loaded RPM)
*(Already a controlled term — see `TERMINOLOGY-LEDGER.md` and Module 07.)* Gross load revenue divided by loaded miles only — the number most commonly advertised on a load board or rate confirmation. A high loaded RPM does not necessarily mean a better load (see Loaded Mile Blindness below).

### Effective RPM (All-In Revenue Per Total Mile)
*(Established in Module 07.)* Total gross load revenue divided by total trip miles — a more complete picture of what a load actually pays per mile driven than loaded RPM alone, since it accounts for deadhead.

### Loaded Mile Blindness
*(Central to Module 07's core teaching.)* The habit of judging a load only by its loaded RPM and ignoring deadhead — the single most common beginner mistake in load evaluation. A load advertised at a higher loaded RPM with heavy deadhead can pay less per total mile than a lower-advertised-rate load with minimal deadhead.

## Section 2: Trip Cost Components

### Total Trip Miles
*(Established in Module 07.)* Origin deadhead plus loaded miles plus relevant destination/repositioning deadhead — the base figure both loaded RPM and effective RPM calculations depend on, though only effective RPM actually uses the full total.

### Estimated Fuel Cost
*(Established in Module 07.)* Total trip miles divided by truck MPG, multiplied by fuel price per gallon — usually the single largest variable cost in a load calculation. Fuel price must come from a current, sourced figure (e.g., the U.S. Energy Information Administration), never a memorized fixed price.

### Tolls / Route-Specific Expenses
*(Established in Module 07.)* Documented costs tied to the specific route — factored into total included variable costs, but route-specific and not assumed from a general average.

### Included Variable Trip Costs
**What it means:** The specific set of costs actually included in a given contribution-margin calculation — e.g., fuel, tolls, dispatcher fee, factoring fee — as opposed to the carrier's full cost structure.
**What it does NOT mean:** The carrier's complete cost picture — driver compensation, maintenance, insurance, equipment payments, permits, taxes, and administrative overhead are all excluded unless separately calculated (see Module 07, Section 10).
**Common misuse:** Presenting a contribution-margin figure to a carrier without stating exactly which costs were included, implying it's a fuller picture than it actually is.
**Operational example:** "This contribution-margin figure includes only fuel, tolls, and dispatcher fee — not driver pay, maintenance, insurance, or equipment costs."

## Section 3: Compensation & Fee Structures

### Dispatcher Compensation / Dispatcher Fee
*(Established in Module 07.)* The agreed fee paid to the dispatcher, commonly structured as a percentage of applicable revenue under the dispatcher services agreement — the actual percentage must come from the real signed agreement, never a memorized "typical" figure.

### Factoring Costs
*(Established in Module 07; see also Billing/Factoring terminology for the fuller factoring-relationship treatment.)* The fee a factoring company charges to purchase a carrier's invoice early — percentage varies by the actual factoring agreement, never assumed.

### Quick-Pay Costs
*(Established in Module 07.)* A fee some brokers or factors charge for expedited payment — distinct from standard factoring; the applicable percentage comes from the actual quick-pay terms in use for that specific transaction.

### Accessorial Revenue / Reimbursements
*(Established in Module 07 and Shipper/Receiver Terminology.)* Additional payments tied to specific events (detention, layover, TONU, lumper reimbursement) when they apply and are documented — never assumed as a fixed universal amount.

## Section 4: Margin, Profit & Break-Even Vocabulary

### Variable Operating Costs
*(Established in Module 07.)* Costs that change with the trip — fuel, tolls, this-load-specific fees — as distinct from fixed/period operating costs.

### Fixed / Period Operating Costs
*(Established in Module 07.)* Costs that exist regardless of any single load — insurance, equipment payments, permits — not calculated per-trip in Module 07's introductory framework.

### Estimated Contribution Margin
*(Established in Module 07 and cross-referenced above.)* Applicable load revenue minus the included variable trip costs for that specific calculation — explicitly NOT the same as net profit. Every time this figure is calculated, the included costs must be stated explicitly.

### Break-Even Concept / Break-Even Cost Per Mile
*(Established in Module 07.)* The cost per mile at which a truck's applicable operating costs are exactly covered, with no margin above or below — genuinely carrier-specific, depending on that carrier's complete cost structure and accounting methodology. A dispatcher does not calculate a carrier's true break-even point from a single load.

### Cash Flow
*(Established in Module 07.)* The actual movement of money in and out over a period — not the same as revenue, contribution margin, or profit.

### Revenue
*(Established in Module 07.)* Money earned from a load before any costs are subtracted — the starting figure for every other calculation in this section, never itself a profitability measure.

### Expense
*(Established in Module 07.)* A cost incurred in earning revenue — must always be paired with a specific category (variable/fixed, included/excluded) rather than used as a vague catch-all.

### Margin
*(Established in Module 07.)* The difference between revenue and a defined set of costs, always with those costs specified — an undefined "margin" figure is not meaningful without stating what was subtracted.

### Accounting Profit
*(Established in Module 07.)* A carrier's full financial result after all recognized costs, calculated using the carrier's own accounting methodology — not something a dispatcher calculates from a single load, and not the same as any of the narrower figures above (contribution margin, cash flow, revenue).

## Section 5: Load Comparison

### Load-Comparison Methodology
*(Established in Module 07, Section 15.)* The sequence for comparing two or more loads properly: calculate total trip miles for each, calculate effective RPM (not loaded RPM alone) for each, estimate fuel cost using actual MPG and current fuel price, identify applicable fees, calculate contribution margin using the same included-cost categories for a fair comparison, and consider next-load positioning as a separate qualitative factor.

### Next-Load Positioning
**What it means:** Where a load's delivery point leaves the truck situated for finding its next load — a qualitative factor in load comparison, separate from the dollar calculation itself.
**What it does NOT mean:** A figure with its own fixed dollar value — positioning is a judgment factor to raise with the carrier alongside the numbers, not itself part of the contribution-margin calculation.
**Common misuse:** Ignoring positioning entirely and comparing loads on dollar figures alone, or conversely, trying to assign positioning a precise dollar value it doesn't actually have.
**Operational example:** "Load B's effective RPM is slightly lower, but it delivers into a high-freight-volume area — worth weighing that positioning advantage alongside the numbers."

### Deadhead Trap
*(Established in Module 07's core worked example.)* The pattern where a load with a higher loaded RPM actually leaves less money after fuel than a lower-advertised-rate load with less deadhead — the concrete illustration of why loaded mile blindness matters.

## Section 6: Introductory Screening (Cross-Referencing Module 05)

### Introductory Rate Screening
*(Established in Module 05, Section 27.)* The limited rate-related evaluation appropriate at the sourcing stage — posted rate, loaded miles, origin deadhead, total trip miles, destination positioning — used only to decide whether a candidate load is worth pursuing into the fuller Module 07 analysis, not a substitute for that fuller analysis.

### "Highest Rate ≠ Best Load"
*(Central teaching point shared by Modules 05 and 07.)* The principle that the highest posted rate, and separately the highest loaded RPM, are not automatically the best load once deadhead, positioning, and full cost structure are considered.
