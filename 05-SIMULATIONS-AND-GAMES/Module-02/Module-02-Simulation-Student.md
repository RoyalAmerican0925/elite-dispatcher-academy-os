## Before You Start

You dispatch for **Meridian Line Freight LLC**. This week brings a series of freight opportunities, requests, and situations that require you to recognize the boundary between ordinary carrier-side dispatching and conduct that presents brokerage risk. You'll analyze **actual conduct**, not labels like "dispatcher," "dispatch service," "bona fide agent," or "carrier representative."

**SIM02-D04 — Supporting Facts** *(select all that apply)*

### Stage 2 Feedback

**SIM02-D03 answer: A.** This is a load matching your one represented carrier's equipment and lanes, offered directly for that carrier. No allocation decision among competing carriers is involved.

If you selected B or C: look at who the load was offered to and whether you're choosing among multiple carriers. Here, it's offered directly for your one represented carrier — the core carrier-side pattern.

If you selected the broker or pay options: those aren't what makes this carrier-side agency. Focus on the offer being for one carrier with no allocation choice required.

## STAGE 3 — Shipper-Direct Request

> **Shipper-Direct Email**
> From: Harlow Manufacturing (shipper)
> To: Dispatch
> "We have three loads this week. You work with several carriers, right? Just pick whichever of your carriers can take each one and let us know."

**SIM02-D05 — Source and Control** *(choice)*

Who supplied this freight opportunity, and who would be selecting which carrier moves each load? Record your choice before checking below.

A. A broker supplied it; the shipper will select the carrier
B. The shipper supplied it directly; the dispatcher would be selecting among carriers
C. Meridian Line Freight supplied it; no selection is needed
D. It's unclear who supplied it

**SIM02-D06 — Classify This Situation** *(choice)*

How should this situation be classified? Record your choice before checking below.

A. Consistent with Carrier-Side Agency — normal dispatching for represented carriers
B. More Facts Required — nothing concerning yet
C. Significant Brokerage Risk — the dispatcher would be exercising discretionary allocation of shipper-supplied freight among multiple represented carriers

**SIM02-D07 — Risk Factors** *(select all that apply)*

Select every fact that contributes to the risk here:

☐ The shipper contacted the dispatch operation directly
☐ Multiple loads are involved
☐ The dispatcher would be choosing which carrier gets each load
☐ The request arrived by email rather than phone

---

### Stage 3 Feedback

**SIM02-D05 answer: B.** The shipper is contacting the dispatch operation directly and explicitly asking the dispatcher to choose among carriers — this is the core fact pattern to watch for.

**SIM02-D06 answer: C.** Receiving freight directly from a shipper and then choosing which of several represented carriers will move it is exactly the allocation-of-traffic pattern that presents significant brokerage risk. STOP and escalate rather than proceeding as ordinary dispatching.

If you selected A or B: this is not ordinary carrier-side coordination and it isn't merely a missing-facts situation — the shipper is asking you to allocate its freight among multiple carriers. That is a serious, recognizable brokerage-risk pattern. **If you miss this a second time, this decision will be flagged for instructor review rather than repeating indefinitely.**

**SIM02-D07 answer:** The first and third options. The number of loads and the communication channel don't drive the classification — who supplied the freight and who chooses the carrier does.

**Proceed to Stage 4.**

## STAGE 4 — Multi-Carrier Decision

> **Carrier-Client Roster**
> Meridian Line Freight LLC — 53-ft dry van — Midwest regional
> Falcon Point Transport — 53-ft dry van — Midwest regional (overlaps with Meridian)
> Redline Freight Co. — 53-ft dry van — Midwest regional (overlaps with Meridian and Falcon Point)

**SIM02-D08 — Overlapping Carriers** *(choice)*

These three carriers all overlap in equipment and territory. Which statement is correct? Record your choice before checking below.

A. Representing all three is automatically fine because you represent carriers, not brokers
B. Representing multiple overlapping carriers is not itself a violation — the risk is if you exercise discretion allocating a single freight opportunity among them
C. Representing more than one carrier is automatically prohibited
D. Overlap in equipment and territory automatically makes this brokerage

> **Carrier-Client Roster (Non-Overlapping)**
> Meridian Line Freight LLC — 53-ft dry van — Midwest regional only
> Summit Ridge Tank Lines — tank trailer — Gulf Coast regional only
> No shared equipment type, no shared operating territory.

**SIM02-D09 — Non-Overlapping Carriers** *(choice)*

Now compare this roster. How should sourcing within each carrier's own standing scope be classified here? Record your choice before checking below.

A. Consistent with Carrier-Side Agency — no overlap means no allocation choice exists between these two carriers for their own freight
B. Significant Brokerage Risk — representing two carriers is inherently risky
C. More Facts Required — cannot classify without knowing compensation

---

### Stage 4 Feedback

**SIM02-D08 answer: B.** Representing multiple carriers — even overlapping ones — is not itself the problem. The risk factor is discretionary allocation of a specific opportunity among them, not the roster structure alone.

If you selected A, C, or D: neither "always fine" nor "automatically prohibited" is correct. The number of carriers or their overlap isn't the test — discretionary allocation of a specific freight opportunity among competing carriers is.

**SIM02-D09 answer: A.** With genuinely non-overlapping equipment and territory, there's no discretionary choice to make between these two carriers for their own standing freight — each stays within its own lane.

If you selected B or C: contrast this with the overlapping roster in the prior decision — here, non-overlap removes the allocation-choice risk. Representing two carriers by itself isn't the issue.

**Proceed to Stage 5.**

## STAGE 5 — Rejected Load Event

> **Carrier Rejection Message**
> From: Meridian Line Freight
> To: Dispatch
> "Can't cover this one — driver's out of hours. You'll have to let the broker know."

**SIM02-D10 — What Do You Do?** *(choice)*

The load originally came from a broker for Meridian specifically. What should you do? Record your choice before checking below.

A. Return the opportunity to its source (the broker) and let them know Meridian can't cover it
B. Reassign the load to a different represented carrier without returning it first
C. Quietly hold onto the load and decide later
D. Assign it to whichever of your other carriers pays the best dispatch fee

> **Compensation Note**
> Falcon Point Transport's dispatch agreement pays a higher per-load fee than Meridian's. This fact is provided for the scenario only — compensation amount does not itself resolve the classification question.

**SIM02-D11 — Why Redirecting Is Risky** *(select all that apply)*

Why is redirecting this load to Falcon Point (a higher-paying carrier) risky, rather than simply returning it?

☐ It involves the dispatcher choosing among carriers for freight that wasn't offered to them directly
☐ Choosing based on which carrier pays the dispatcher more adds a self-interested allocation motive on top of the discretion itself
☐ It might get the load delivered faster
☐ The broker doesn't need to know about the reassignment

---

### Stage 5 Feedback

**SIM02-D10 answer: A.** Returning a declined opportunity to its source is materially different from taking control of it and reallocating it yourself. Returning it is the low-risk path; reallocating it is where the risk begins.

If you selected B, C, or D: declining and returning an opportunity to its source is not the same as taking control of it and choosing a different carrier yourself. The latter starts to look like allocation of traffic. **If you miss this a second time, this decision will be flagged for instructor review rather than repeating indefinitely.**

**SIM02-D11 answer:** The first two options. Delivery speed and whether the broker finds out aren't the controlling factors — the discretionary carrier choice, especially compensation-motivated, is what creates the risk.

**Proceed to Stage 6.**

**SIM02-D13 — Classify the Actual Conduct** *(choice)*

Now compare the actual conduct described here against the "bona fide agent" label. How should this be classified? Record your choice before checking below.

A. Consistent with Carrier-Side Agency — the contract label settles it
B. Significant Brokerage Risk — receiving shipper freight first, then choosing among unrelated represented carriers afterward, is the allocation-of-traffic pattern regardless of the label

### Stage 6 Feedback

**SIM02-D12 answer: B.** A label can be supporting evidence, but it does not by itself decide the classification — what actually happens in practice controls.

If you selected A, C, or D: neither extreme is right. The label isn't meaningless, but it also isn't the final word. Conduct is what actually decides this.

**SIM02-D13 answer: B.** This conduct — receiving the shipper's freight first, then deciding afterward which unrelated carrier will move it — matches the same allocation-of-traffic risk pattern seen in Stage 3, no matter what the contract is titled.

If you selected A, C, or D: the label says "bona fide agent," but the conduct described is the same risk pattern already seen with the shipper-direct scenario. Don't let the label override what's actually happening. **If you miss this a second time, this decision will be flagged for instructor review rather than repeating indefinitely.**

## STAGE 7 — Final Boundary Decision

> **Freight Opportunity Sheet (Incomplete)**
> A represented carrier's dispatcher is contacted about a load. Some details are known: the carrier has an ongoing relationship and a preexisting agreement.
> Missing: whether this specific load was offered directly to this carrier alone, or whether the dispatcher had any role in choosing among carriers for it.

**SIM02-D14 — What's the Correct Response?** *(choice)*

Key facts are missing from this file. What is the correct response? Record your choice before checking below.

A. Assume it's fine since the carrier has an ongoing relationship
B. Assume it's risky since shipper-direct contact is often risky
C. STOP — identify exactly which facts are missing, check them before concluding, and escalate if they can't be resolved
D. Guess based on which classification seems more common in this simulation

**SIM02-D15 — Sequence the Response** *(sequencing)*

Put the correct response steps in order for this situation:

1. IDENTIFY exactly which facts are missing (was this offered to one carrier alone? was any allocation choice involved?)
2. STOP — do not proceed or classify yet
3. CHECK the missing facts through an appropriate source before concluding
4. ESCALATE if the facts cannot be resolved or the classification remains uncertain

---

### Stage 7 Feedback

**SIM02-D14 answer: C.** With controlling facts missing — specifically, whether this load was offered directly for this carrier alone, or whether any allocation choice was involved — the correct move is STOP → IDENTIFY → CHECK → ESCALATE, not a guess in either direction.

If you selected A, B, or D: an ongoing relationship doesn't resolve this specific load's facts, and shipper-direct contact isn't automatically risky either — Stage 2 showed a safe carrier-side pattern too. When controlling facts are missing, identify and check them — don't guess.

**SIM02-D15 answer:** STOP → IDENTIFY → CHECK → ESCALATE. Stop first, so you don't proceed on an incomplete picture. Then identify precisely what's missing. Then check it through an appropriate source. Then escalate if it's still unresolved.

If your sequence differed: the order matters — stop before anything else, then identify the specific gap, then check it, and only escalate if checking doesn't resolve it.
