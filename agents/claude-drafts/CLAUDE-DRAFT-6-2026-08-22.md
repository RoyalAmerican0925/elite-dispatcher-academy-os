# Dispatcher vs. Broker: Know the Regulatory Boundary

---

## Why This Distinction Matters

One of the most important things you will learn as a professional dispatcher is where your legal authority begins and ends. The trucking industry operates under a detailed federal regulatory framework, and the line between dispatching and brokering is not just a technicality — it carries real legal and financial consequences.

Understanding this boundary protects you, the carriers you work with, and the shippers who depend on reliable freight movement. This section explains the key definitions, how they apply to your daily work, and what to watch for as you build your dispatching career.

---

## The Regulatory Foundation

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) is the federal agency that oversees commercial motor carrier operations in the United States. The definitions that govern this discussion come directly from federal regulation.

**49 CFR § 371.2** contains the official definitions of a property broker and a bona fide agent. These definitions are the legal starting point for understanding what you can and cannot do as a dispatcher.

In **June 2023**, FMCSA issued final guidance that further clarified these definitions — specifically addressing broker and bona fide agent status as it applies to dispatch-service activities. This guidance was significant for the dispatching industry because it made clear that the label "dispatcher" does not automatically place someone outside the scope of broker regulations. The function of what you do — not just what you call yourself — determines how federal rules apply to your work.

---

## Key Definitions in Plain Language

**Property Broker:** A person or company that, for compensation, arranges transportation of freight by motor carriers. A property broker does not take possession of the freight and does not operate the trucks. A broker acts as the middle party connecting shippers with carriers.

**Bona Fide Agent:** A person who acts on behalf of a motor carrier as a true representative of that carrier — not as an independent middle party. A bona fide agent works within the carrier's authority, not independently.

**Carrier:** A motor carrier that actually transports freight using its own operating authority and equipment (or leased equipment under its authority).

**Dispatcher (as a service provider):** A person or company that provides administrative and operational support to motor carriers — such as finding loads, handling paperwork, and communicating with brokers or shippers — while acting on behalf of the carrier, not independently arranging transportation for compensation as a separate party.

## Comparison Table

| Role | Arranges Transportation for Compensation? | Holds Own Operating Authority? | Requires Broker Authority? | Financial Security Required? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Carrier** | No — performs transportation | Yes — MC number / operating authority | No | Varies by carrier type |
| **Dispatcher / Bona Fide Agent** | Acts on behalf of carrier only | No independent authority | No — if truly acting as carrier's agent | No |
| **Broker** | Yes — arranges for others | No — does not transport freight | Yes — FMCSA broker authority required | Yes — $75,000 via BMC-84 or BMC-85 |

---

## The Broker Authority Requirement

If a person or company is found to be functioning as a property broker — regardless of what they call themselves — they are required to obtain FMCSA broker authority and maintain **$75,000 in financial security**. This financial security must be held through either a **BMC-84** (surety bond) or a **BMC-85** (trust fund agreement).

This requirement exists to protect shippers and carriers from financial harm. It is not a formality — operating as an unlicensed broker is a federal violation.

---

> ⚠️ **CAUTION**
>
> Calling yourself a "dispatcher" does not automatically exempt you from broker regulations. If your activities involve independently arranging transportation between shippers and carriers for compensation — rather than acting as a true agent of a specific carrier — federal regulators may classify your work as brokering. Dispatching is not a loophole around broker registration requirements. If you are uncertain about how your business model is classified, consult a transportation attorney familiar with FMCSA regulations before operating.
