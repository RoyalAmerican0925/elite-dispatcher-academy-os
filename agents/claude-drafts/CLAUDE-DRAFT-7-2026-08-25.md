# Dispatcher vs. Broker: Know the Regulatory Boundary

---

## Why This Distinction Matters

One of the most important things you will learn as a dispatcher is where your legal role begins and where it ends. The federal government draws a clear line between carriers, dispatchers (also called bona fide agents), and brokers. Crossing that line without the proper authority is not a technicality — it is a federal compliance issue that can result in fines, loss of operating authority, and legal liability.

This section explains what each role means under federal regulation, how they compare, and how to recognize the boundary in real-world situations.

---

## The Regulatory Foundation

The primary federal regulation governing this topic is **49 CFR § 371.2**, which contains the official definitions for both "broker" and "bona fide agent" as recognized by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA).

In **June 2023**, FMCSA issued final guidance that further clarified these definitions — specifically addressing how dispatch-service activities fit within the existing regulatory framework. This guidance was significant for the dispatching industry because it confirmed that the definitions in 49 CFR § 371.2 apply to dispatch services, and that the title someone uses for themselves does not automatically determine their regulatory category.

In other words, calling yourself a "dispatcher" does not automatically exempt you from broker requirements. What matters is what you actually do.

---

## Understanding the Three Roles

### The Carrier
A carrier is the entity that physically transports freight. Carriers hold operating authority issued by FMCSA and are directly responsible for the movement of goods. When a carrier hires someone to help manage their loads, communications, and logistics internally, that person may function as a bona fide agent of that carrier.

### The Dispatcher / Bona Fide Agent
A dispatcher who operates as a bona fide agent works on behalf of a specific carrier — not independently between shippers and multiple carriers. The bona fide agent relationship means the dispatcher is acting as an extension of the carrier they represent. This is a key distinction. The dispatcher's authority flows from the carrier's authority, and the dispatcher does not independently arrange transportation between unrelated parties for compensation in a way that constitutes brokering.

### The Broker
A property broker arranges transportation of freight for compensation by connecting shippers with carriers. Brokers operate independently and must hold their own FMCSA broker authority. They are also required to maintain **$75,000 in financial security**, filed through either a **BMC-84** (surety bond) or **BMC-85** (trust fund agreement). These requirements exist to protect shippers and carriers in the event of financial disputes or non-payment.

## Comparison Table

| Feature | Carrier | Dispatcher / Bona Fide Agent | Broker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physically moves freight | Yes | No | No |
| Holds FMCSA operating authority | Yes (motor carrier) | No (operates under carrier's authority) | Yes (broker authority required) |
| Arranges freight for compensation | No (moves it directly) | Acts on behalf of one carrier | Yes, between shippers and carriers |
| $75,000 financial security required | No | No | Yes (BMC-84 or BMC-85) |
| Regulated under 49 CFR § 371.2 | No | Yes (bona fide agent definition) | Yes (broker definition) |
| Works independently between multiple parties | No | No | Yes |

---

> ⚠️ **CAUTION**
>
> Dispatching is not a loophole around broker registration requirements. If your activities involve independently arranging transportation between shippers and carriers — especially across multiple carriers you do not represent as a bona fide agent — you may be functioning as a broker under federal regulation, regardless of what you call yourself. The FMCSA June 2023 guidance makes clear that the substance of your activities, not your job title, determines your regulatory category. When in doubt, consult a transportation attorney before proceeding.

## Three Real-World Scenarios

### Scenario 1: The Owner-Operator's Dispatcher
Maria works exclusively for one owner-operator. She finds loads, communicates with brokers on his behalf, handles paperwork, and manages his schedule. She does not work with any other carriers and does not independently negotiate freight arrangements between shippers and multiple trucking companies. In this situation, Maria is functioning as a bona fide agent of that carrier. She is operating within the dispatcher role as defined under 49 CFR § 371.2.

### Scenario 2: The Multi-Carrier Dispatch Service
James runs a dispatch service and works with 15 different owner-operators simultaneously. He contacts brokers and shippers, selects loads, and negotiates rates on behalf of whichever carrier he thinks is the best fit for a given load — sometimes steering freight toward one carrier over another based on his own judgment and business relationships. James should carefully evaluate whether his activities have crossed into brokering territory. Independently arranging transportation between shippers and multiple carriers for compensation is the functional definition of brokering, and FMCSA's 2023 guidance applies directly to situations like his.

### Scenario 3: The Shipper's Request
A small manufacturing company contacts a dispatcher directly and asks her to find a carrier to move their freight. The dispatcher is not representing any specific carrier in this transaction — she is acting as the intermediary, selecting from available carriers and arranging the shipment for a fee paid by the shipper. This activity closely resembles brokering. Without FMCSA broker authority and the required $75,000 financial security, this dispatcher may be operating outside her legal authority.
