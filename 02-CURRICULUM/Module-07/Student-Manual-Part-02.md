# 6. Worked Example 2 — The Deadhead Trap

**FICTIONAL INSTRUCTIONAL ASSUMPTIONS:**

**Load A**

* Revenue: $1,200
* Loaded Miles: 400
* Relevant Deadhead: 200
* Fuel Efficiency: 6 MPG
* Illustrative Fuel Price: $3.50/gallon

Total Miles: 400 + 200 = **600**
Loaded RPM: $1,200 ÷ 400 = **$3.00**
Effective RPM: $1,200 ÷ 600 = **$2.00**
Fuel: 600 ÷ 6 = 100 gallons × $3.50 = **$350**
Revenue after fuel only: $1,200 − $350 = **$850**

**Load B**

* Revenue: $1,150
* Loaded Miles: 460
* Deadhead: 0
* Fuel Efficiency: 6 MPG
* Illustrative Fuel Price: $3.50/gallon

Total Miles: **460**
Loaded RPM: $1,150 ÷ 460 = **$2.50**
Effective RPM: $1,150 ÷ 460 = **$2.50**
Estimated Fuel: 460 ÷ 6 = approximately 76.67 gallons × $3.50 = approximately **$268.33**
Revenue after fuel only: $1,150 − $268.33 = approximately **$881.67**

**Teach explicitly:** Load A *looks* better using loaded RPM alone — $3.00 versus $2.50. But once relevant deadhead and fuel are factored in, Load B leaves more money after fuel ($881.67 versus $850) despite its lower advertised rate. Do not call either calculation "final profit" — this compares fuel-adjusted revenue only, not full contribution margin or net profit. The lesson is about which number you look at first, not a claim that Load B is definitively "better" in every respect.

# 7. Loaded Mile Blindness

"Loaded mile blindness" is the habit of judging a load only by its loaded RPM and ignoring deadhead. It is the single most common beginner mistake in load evaluation.

A load advertised at $3.00/loaded-mile with heavy deadhead on both ends can pay less per total mile driven than a load advertised at $2.50/loaded-mile with little or no deadhead. The advertised rate is a starting point for evaluation, not the answer.

**Do Not Assume:** A higher loaded RPM automatically means a more profitable load. Always calculate effective RPM before comparing two loads.

# 8. Deadhead Analysis

Deadhead is unpaid mileage, but it is not automatically "wasted" — it still costs fuel, time, and hours-of-service, and it must be included in any honest evaluation of a load's economics.

Two deadhead concepts matter:

* **Origin deadhead** — the empty miles to reach the pickup. This is usually the more obvious cost, since it happens before revenue starts.
* **Destination/repositioning deadhead** — empty miles after delivery when relevant to evaluating that load's true cost, particularly if the delivery location leaves the truck poorly positioned for the next load. Whether to count this, and how much, depends on the carrier's own planning horizon and is a judgment call to raise with the carrier — not a fixed rule.

A load with minimal deadhead on both ends will often out-perform a nominally higher-paying load that requires significant empty running, once the numbers are actually run.

# 9. Fuel-Cost Estimation

Fuel is usually the single largest variable cost in a load calculation. The formula is straightforward — total trip miles divided by the truck's MPG, multiplied by fuel price per gallon — but two inputs deserve care:

* **MPG varies.** Loaded weight, terrain, weather, idling, and truck condition all affect real-world fuel efficiency. A carrier's own historical average MPG for that truck is far more useful than a generic industry figure.
* **Fuel prices change constantly and vary by region.** Do not memorize a fixed fuel price as if it were permanent. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes current U.S. on-highway diesel price data and is an appropriate source to check for current pricing — see Section 17 for the source note. Never hard-code a specific fuel price into your evaluation habits as if it were a fixed truth; always use a current, sourced figure when the calculation matters for a real decision.

# 10. Carrier-Specific Costs

This module teaches you to calculate *illustrative* variable costs — fuel, tolls, dispatcher fees, factoring fees — using clearly labeled assumptions. It does not teach you the carrier's actual full cost structure, because that structure is specific to each carrier and includes categories this module does not calculate:

* Driver compensation (mileage pay, percentage pay, or salary — carrier-specific)
* Maintenance and repair allocation
* Tires
* DEF (diesel exhaust fluid)
* Insurance
* Equipment payments (truck/trailer financing or leasing)
* Permits and licensing
* Taxes
* Administrative overhead

**Do Not Assume:** That the "included variable trip costs" calculated in this module represent the carrier's full cost picture. They do not. A dispatcher who wants to understand a specific carrier's fuller cost structure should ask that carrier directly, using tools like the Carrier Onboarding Cost-Information Worksheet referenced in Section 16.

# 11. Contribution Margin

Estimated contribution margin, as calculated in this module, is:

```
Applicable Load Revenue − Included Variable Trip Costs = Estimated Contribution Margin
```

This tells you what's left after the *specific* variable costs you included — nothing more. It is a genuinely useful number for comparing two loads apples-to-apples, because both calculations use the same limited cost categories. It is not a substitute for the carrier's actual profit-and-loss picture, which would also need to account for fixed costs, driver pay, and everything else listed in Section 10.

**IMPORTANT:** Every time you present a contribution-margin figure to a carrier, state plainly which costs were included and which were not. Presenting an incomplete calculation as if it were final profit is misleading, even if unintentional.

# 12. Break-Even Concept

Break-even cost per mile is the point where a truck's applicable operating costs exactly equal its revenue per mile — no margin, no loss.

```
Applicable Operating Costs ÷ Applicable Miles = Break-Even Cost Per Mile
```

This module does not teach you to calculate a specific carrier's true break-even point, because that number depends on that carrier's complete cost structure and accounting methodology — the same categories listed in Section 10, calculated over the carrier's actual operating period, not a single load. What this module teaches is the *concept*: that every carrier has some cost-per-mile threshold below which a load loses money, and that a dispatcher who understands the concept can have a more informed conversation with a carrier about it, without claiming to calculate the carrier's exact number.

Industry research organizations such as the American Transportation Research Institute (ATRI) publish national average operating-cost data that can be useful for general context. **Do not** treat a national average as if it were a specific carrier's break-even figure — individual carrier economics vary widely and control the actual load-level decision.
