# 13. Accessorial Economics

Accessorial payments — detention, layover, TONU (truck-ordered-not-used), lumper reimbursement, extra-stop fees — can meaningfully change a load's real economics. But this module will not teach you fixed dollar amounts or fixed time thresholds for any of them, because none of them are universal.

**Do Not Assume:**

* That detention always begins after two hours.
* That detention has a standard hourly rate.
* That TONU has a standard amount.
* That lumpers are always reimbursed.
* That dispatchers always exclude lumper fees from their commission calculation.
* That extra stops always pay a particular amount.
* That layover automatically pays.
* That accessorial payment is automatic just because a delay occurred.

**What to verify instead, every time:**

* The rate confirmation for this specific load.
* The applicable broker/carrier agreement.
* The dispatcher's own agreement with the carrier (does dispatcher commission apply to accessorials, or only to linehaul?).
* Receipts (for lumper or other reimbursable expenses).
* Arrival/departure records (to support any detention or layover claim).
* Detention documentation specifically required by the broker or shipper.
* Any other applicable written terms.

Accessorial income can be real and can matter to a load's economics — but it is documentation-dependent, not a number you should ever assume in advance.

# 14. Factoring and Quick-Pay Economics

Factoring and quick-pay both trade a fee for faster access to cash. Neither has a fixed "standard" rate — do not teach or memorize:

* 5–10% dispatcher fees
* 1–5% factoring fees
* 3–5% quick-pay fees

as if they were industry norms. Percentages in this module are used only as clearly labeled fictional mathematical assumptions for practicing the formula. In a real scenario, the applicable percentage comes from the carrier's actual factoring agreement or the broker/factor's actual quick-pay terms — always sourced from the real document, never assumed.

The formula itself doesn't change:

```
Applicable Invoice Amount × Contractual Fee Percentage = Estimated Fee
```

What changes, every time, is where the percentage comes from.

# 15. Load-Comparison Methodology

When comparing two or more loads, follow this sequence rather than comparing loaded RPM alone:

1. Calculate total trip miles for each load (including relevant deadhead).
2. Calculate effective RPM for each load, not just loaded RPM.
3. Estimate fuel cost for each load using the truck's actual MPG and a current fuel price.
4. Identify any applicable dispatcher fee, factoring fee, or tolls for each load.
5. Calculate estimated contribution margin for each load, using the same included-cost categories for both so the comparison is apples-to-apples.
6. Consider next-load positioning — does one load leave the truck somewhere with better or worse onward opportunities? This is a judgment factor, not a formula.
7. Present the comparison to the carrier with all assumptions stated, and let the carrier make the final call.

# 16. Dispatcher Decision Workflow

A repeatable operational sequence for evaluating a proposed load:

1. Load candidate identified and broker/rate confirmation verified (Module 06).
2. Gross load revenue and loaded miles captured from the rate confirmation.
3. Origin deadhead calculated from truck's current location.
4. Destination/repositioning deadhead estimated if relevant to this evaluation.
5. Total trip miles calculated.
6. Loaded RPM and effective RPM both calculated.
7. Fuel cost estimated using current MPG and current fuel price.
8. Tolls and other known route-specific costs identified.
9. Applicable dispatcher fee and factoring/quick-pay fee calculated per the actual agreements in place.
10. Estimated contribution margin calculated, with included costs stated explicitly.
11. If comparing multiple loads, Section 15's methodology applied to each.
12. Numbers presented to the carrier, with every assumption labeled — the carrier makes the final accept/decline decision.

Reference tools: Calculation Worksheet, Load-Comparison Worksheet, and Carrier Onboarding Cost-Information Worksheet (Section 20 and `04-STUDENT-TOOLS/Module-07-Tools/`).

# 17. Realistic Dispatch Scenarios

Every scenario below uses clearly labeled fictional instructional assumptions. None represent current market rates, current fuel prices, or standard accessorial amounts.

**Scenario A — Short Haul vs. Long Haul**
Short-haul load: $450 revenue, 150 loaded miles, 20 miles deadhead, MPG 6.5, fuel $3.75. Total miles 170; Loaded RPM $3.00; Effective RPM $2.65; fuel ≈ 26.15 gal × $3.75 ≈ $98.08.
Long-haul load: $2,100 revenue, 900 loaded miles, 40 miles deadhead, MPG 7, fuel $3.75. Total miles 940; Loaded RPM $2.33; Effective RPM $2.23; fuel ≈ 134.29 gal × $3.75 ≈ $503.57.
Teaching point: short-haul loads often show a higher RPM but smaller absolute dollars after costs; long-haul loads often show a lower RPM but larger absolute contribution. Neither is automatically "better" — it depends on the carrier's weekly revenue goals and hours available.

**Scenario B — Excessive Origin Deadhead**
Revenue $1,400, loaded miles 500, origin deadhead 350 miles, MPG 6.5, fuel $3.80. Total miles 850; Loaded RPM $2.80; Effective RPM $1.65. Teaching point: heavy origin deadhead can turn an attractive loaded RPM into a mediocre effective RPM. Always calculate before accepting.

**Scenario C — Destination Positioning**
Load delivers into a low-freight-volume area with likely 150+ miles of deadhead to the next viable load, versus a competing load that delivers into a high-freight-volume area with likely under 50 miles of deadhead to the next load. Teaching point: even if both loads show similar effective RPM at time of booking, destination positioning affects the *next* load's economics — worth raising with the carrier as a planning factor, not a hard number.

**Scenario D — Toll Route vs. Non-Toll Route**
Same load, same revenue and miles, Route 1 has $65 in tolls, Route 2 adds 40 miles but no tolls. Teaching point: calculate fuel cost for the longer no-toll route before assuming it's cheaper — sometimes the toll route is actually less expensive once extra fuel is accounted for.

**Scenario E — Detention**
A load's rate confirmation states detention pays after 2 free hours at $50/hour, documented by signed BOL timestamps. This is that specific load's documented term — not a universal rule (see Section 13). Teaching point: only apply detention math when the specific documentation supports it.

**Scenario F — Layover**
A driver is held overnight by the receiver. The dispatcher checks the rate confirmation and carrier agreement; layover pay is not mentioned. Teaching point: do not assume layover pay exists — verify, and if it isn't documented, don't build it into the load's expected revenue.

**Scenario G — TONU (Truck Ordered Not Used)**
A shipper cancels after the truck has already deadheaded 80 miles to the pickup. The dispatcher checks the rate confirmation for a TONU clause before assuming any payment applies. Teaching point: TONU amounts and even TONU eligibility are agreement-specific, never assumed.

**Scenario H — Lumper Expense**
A lumper fee of $150 is paid at delivery. The carrier's agreement states lumper reimbursements are excluded from dispatcher commission. Teaching point: always check whether accessorial reimbursements are included or excluded from the dispatcher fee base before calculating that fee.

**Scenario I — Multi-Stop Freight**
A load has one pickup and two deliveries, adding 45 miles of inter-stop travel beyond direct-route mileage. Teaching point: multi-stop mileage must be included in total trip miles — using only origin-to-final-destination mileage understates the true trip length.

**Scenario J — Dispatcher Compensation Applied**
Revenue $1,800, dispatcher fee 6% per the actual dispatcher agreement on file. Dispatcher fee = $108. Teaching point: always pull the percentage from the actual signed agreement, never a memorized "typical" number.

**Scenario K — Factoring Applied**
Invoice $1,800, factoring fee 2.5% per the carrier's actual factoring agreement. Fee = $45. Teaching point: same principle as Scenario J — the real agreement controls, not an assumed industry rate.

**Scenario L — Quick-Pay Applied**
Same invoice, carrier instead chooses a broker quick-pay option at 3% for payment in 24 hours instead of standard terms. Fee = $54. Teaching point: quick-pay and factoring are different tools solving the same cash-flow problem — verify which one actually applies to this transaction.

**Scenario M — Carrier-Specific Break-Even**
Carrier reports (via its own records, not this module's calculation) that its all-in cost per mile is $1.85. A proposed load's effective RPM is $1.70. Teaching point: even a load with reasonable-looking numbers can fall below a specific carrier's real break-even point — this is exactly why the carrier's own cost structure matters more than any generic assumption.

**Scenario N — Two Competing Loads**
Load 1: Effective RPM $2.10, contribution margin $780 (given assumptions). Load 2: Effective RPM $1.95, contribution margin $845 (given assumptions, due to lower deadhead). Teaching point: present both effective RPM and contribution margin — a lower RPM can still mean more dollars, exactly as in the Deadhead Trap example.

**Scenario O — Three Competing Loads**
Load 1: Effective RPM $2.20, positions truck poorly for next load. Load 2: Effective RPM $2.05, positions truck well. Load 3: Effective RPM $1.90, includes a documented $200 detention allowance already earned on a prior stop this trip. Teaching point: with three options, the highest single-number metric is rarely the whole story — bring the carrier the full comparison, not just a ranked list.

**Scenario P — High-Paying Load That Performs Poorly**
Revenue $2,400, loaded miles 500, deadhead 450 miles (both ends combined), MPG 6, fuel $3.90. Total miles 950; Loaded RPM $4.80 (looks excellent); Effective RPM $2.53; fuel ≈ 158.33 gal × $3.90 ≈ $617.50. Teaching point: an eye-catching loaded RPM can hide enormous deadhead. Always run the full calculation before getting excited about an advertised rate.

**Scenario Q — Lower-Paying Load That Positions the Truck Better**
Revenue $1,050, loaded miles 350, deadhead 10 miles, MPG 6, fuel $3.90, and the delivery point is a high-freight-volume area. Total miles 360; Loaded RPM $3.00; Effective RPM $2.92; fuel ≈ 60 gal × $3.90 = $234. Teaching point: a modest-looking load with minimal deadhead and strong next-load positioning can be a better business decision than a bigger number with poor positioning — bring both the math and the positioning context to the carrier.

# 18. Common Mistakes

* Comparing loads using loaded RPM only, ignoring deadhead (loaded mile blindness).
* Treating contribution margin as if it were net profit.
* Assuming a fixed accessorial amount without checking documentation.
* Using a memorized "typical" dispatcher fee, factoring fee, or fuel price instead of the actual applicable figures.
* Ignoring destination positioning when comparing otherwise-similar loads.
* Failing to include multi-stop mileage in total trip miles.
* Presenting a partial cost calculation to a carrier without stating which costs were and weren't included.

# 19. Do Not Assume — Summary Box

* Do not assume a higher loaded RPM means a better load.
* Do not assume contribution margin equals net profit.
* Do not assume any accessorial amount, threshold, or eligibility without documentation.
* Do not assume a fixed dispatcher fee, factoring fee, or quick-pay fee — always use the actual agreement.
* Do not assume a national average operating-cost figure is any specific carrier's break-even point.
* Do not assume a fixed fuel price — check a current, sourced figure.

# 24. Module Summary

This module taught you to evaluate a proposed load mathematically: calculating total trip miles, loaded RPM versus effective RPM, estimated fuel cost, dispatcher and factoring fees, and estimated contribution margin — while being explicit, every time, about which costs were and weren't included. The goal is not to replace the carrier's business judgment — it's to bring the carrier accurate, clearly-labeled numbers so they can make an informed decision.
