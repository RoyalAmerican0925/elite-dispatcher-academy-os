# MODULE 08 — RATE NEGOTIATION AND BROKER COMMUNICATION
## Student Manual — Part 02: Pre-Negotiation Intelligence

## Purpose
The best time to discover that a negotiation position makes no sense is **before** you make the call.

Part 02 teaches the preparation desk: convert verified opportunity facts, carrier constraints and Module 07 economics into a controlled negotiating position.

**PREPARE → VERIFY FACTS → SET POSITION** comes before OPEN → QUESTION → LISTEN → COUNTER.

---

## 1. Build from facts, not excitement

A load-board rate or broker offer can create urgency. Urgency is not analysis.

Before negotiating, assemble the facts that materially affect the carrier's decision. Depending on the supplied case, these can include:
- carrier and truck/driver being considered;
- current truck position and availability;
- deadhead to pickup;
- loaded movement;
- relevant repositioning or other total-trip miles supplied by the Module 07 method;
- pickup and delivery requirements;
- equipment requirement;
- commodity/weight information relevant to the current decision;
- stated rate;
- known agreement-specific charges or conditions;
- supplied carrier target/floor/approval rules;
- verified alternative opportunities;
- material missing facts.

Do not use a number merely because it is present. If a fact is material and uncertain, classify it **VERIFY BEFORE COUNTER**.

---

## 2. Import Module 07 economics correctly

Module 08 does not reteach profitability mathematics. It uses the output.

Suppose Module 07 analysis of a fictional load shows:
- offered gross rate: $2,100;
- total trip miles under the supplied calculation method: 760;
- carrier's supplied minimum economic position for this case: $2,250;
- desired target: $2,400.

Module 08 asks: **How should the dispatcher communicate and negotiate from those results?**

It does not allow the student to throw away the total-trip analysis and negotiate only from loaded miles because the loaded-mile RPM sounds better.

### Operational principle
**Negotiation cannot repair bad arithmetic.**

If the economic input is wrong, the negotiating position can be wrong.

---

## 3. Separate economic requirement from opening counter

The floor is not automatically the first counter.

Example:
- offered: $2,050;
- target: $2,400;
- supplied floor: $2,250.

Opening at $2,250 immediately exposes the lowest supplied acceptable position and leaves no planned concession room.

A prepared dispatcher knows:
- where the conversation starts;
- where the carrier wants it to finish;
- what movement is authorized;
- where authority stops.

The exact strategy depends on supplied carrier policy and case facts. The Academy does not teach a universal percentage markup or universal “always ask $X more” rule.

---

## 4. Position quality depends on fact quality

Consider two opportunities with the same offered rate.

### Opportunity A
- truck 12 miles from pickup;
- pickup window confirmed;
- delivery appointment confirmed;
- equipment match already screened;
- verified broker/contact handoff from prior modules;
- total-trip economics complete.

### Opportunity B
- truck location copied from an old note;
- pickup says “morning” with no window;
- delivery time missing;
- 150 miles of possible deadhead not reconciled;
- rate appears in a forwarded message;
- broker contact not yet authenticated under the applicable workflow.

The same dollar offer does not create the same negotiation readiness.

Opportunity B may require **VERIFY BEFORE COUNTER** rather than immediate negotiation.

---

## 5. Build the negotiation fact sheet

Before calling, answer five groups of questions.

### A. Carrier position
- Which carrier am I representing?
- Which truck/driver is this for?
- What supplied restrictions apply?
- What negotiating authority do I have?

### B. Load facts
- Origin/destination?
- Pickup/delivery timing?
- Equipment/commodity/weight facts needed for this discussion?
- Special stated requirements?
- Which facts remain uncertain?

### C. Economics
- What Module 07 total-trip result applies?
- What is the supplied target?
- What is the supplied floor/minimum?
- What condition triggers walk-away or approval?

### D. Alternatives and leverage
- Is there a real competing opportunity?
- Is the truck position advantageous?
- Has the broker stated urgency or another constraint?
- Which leverage points are verified rather than assumed?

### E. Conversation objective
- What information do I still need?
- What counter will I open with?
- What movement can I make?
- What would cause me to pause, decline or escalate?

---

## 6. Missing facts can change the rate conversation

Suppose a broker offers $2,300 and the supplied floor is $2,250. It looks acceptable.

Then you discover:
- delivery is not next morning as assumed;
- it is a fixed appointment the following evening;
- the supplied carrier case says the extended commitment changes its required economic position.

A dispatcher who accepted first and asked later negotiated from an incomplete picture.

The correct discipline is not “ask every possible question before saying hello.” It is to identify **material facts**—facts capable of changing fit, economics, authority or the decision.

---

## 7. Alternatives: BATNA without jargon dependence

Negotiation literature often uses BATNA—best alternative to a negotiated agreement. You do not need the acronym to use the logic.

Ask:
> If we do not take this opportunity, what realistic verified alternative do we have?

An actual alternative can affect willingness to walk away.

But an imaginary alternative is not leverage.

### Example
Carrier has two verified opportunities:
- Load A: broker offer $2,200, strong total-trip economics, preferred destination.
- Load B: broker offer $2,350, but substantial deadhead and weaker total-trip result.

The higher gross rate is not automatically the better alternative. Module 07 economics still matters.

## 8. Preparation Drill — three opportunities

All facts are fictional training data.

### Truck profile
- empty in Fort Worth at 08:00;
- dry van;
- carrier-supplied minimum position varies by total-trip analysis;
- dispatcher may not accept below the supplied case floor without approval.

### Load A
- pickup Dallas 10:00–13:00;
- delivery Tulsa next morning;
- offer $1,450;
- Module 07 supplied floor $1,500;
- target $1,650;
- all material facts for negotiation confirmed.

### Load B
- pickup “DFW tomorrow”;
- delivery Kansas City;
- offer $1,800;
- exact pickup facility/time missing;
- deadhead cannot yet be established;
- no final Module 07 position supplied.

### Load C
- pickup Denton 11:00;
- delivery Oklahoma City 18:00;
- offer $1,350;
- supplied floor $1,300;
- target $1,450;
- another verified opportunity exists but produces a weaker supplied total-trip result.

For each load:
1. classify PREPARED or VERIFY BEFORE COUNTER;
2. identify offered rate, target and floor where known;
3. identify truthful leverage;
4. identify missing facts;
5. state the opening objective;
6. state the condition that would require escalation.

## 9. Common preparation failures

### Failure: loaded-mile tunnel vision
The dispatcher ignores deadhead/other supplied total-trip miles because the loaded RPM looks attractive.

**Correction:** use the Module 07 result required by the carrier/case.

### Failure: negotiating without a floor
The dispatcher decides in real time how low to go.

**Correction:** establish supplied authority before bargaining.

### Failure: treating target as guaranteed market value
A carrier target is a negotiating objective, not proof that the market must pay it.

### Failure: treating online market data as timeless truth
Market conditions and platform data change. Use current, properly sourced information when the carrier's process requires it.

### Failure: inventing urgency
Do not tell the broker the truck “has to move now” unless that is true and strategically appropriate.

### Failure: no exit condition
A dispatcher keeps negotiating long after the opportunity has failed the carrier's supplied requirements.

**Correction:** know when to DECLINE or ESCALATE.

---

## 10. Preparation output

A complete preparation record should allow another authorized reviewer to understand:
- what opportunity was being negotiated;
- what facts were relied upon;
- what remained uncertain;
- what economic position was supplied;
- what authority the dispatcher had;
- what leverage was real;
- what the opening objective was;
- where the stop/escalation point was.

If the dispatcher cannot explain those points, the call is not yet professionally prepared.
