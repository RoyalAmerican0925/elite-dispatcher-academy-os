**ELITE DISPATCHER ACADEMY**

**Student Manual — Module 05**

Load Boards & Freight Sourcing

# 1. Module Introduction

You now know the industry, your legal boundaries, your equipment, and how to onboard a carrier and driver properly. This module answers the next practical question: where do you actually find freight, how do you read a posting correctly, and how do you decide — quickly — whether a load is worth pursuing for this specific carrier, driver, and equipment?

**IMPORTANT:** Core principle for this entire module: A LOAD POSTING IS A LEAD. It does not automatically prove the freight is still available, that all details are complete, that the carrier is qualified for it, that the broker is legitimate, that the rate is acceptable, or that the load is booked. Every posting starts as a lead and has to earn its way through several stages before it becomes a booked load.

The professional skill this module builds isn't "call every load." It's operating a repeatable sourcing desk: translating a real truck profile into search criteria, triaging results, eliminating obvious bad fits, identifying missing or conflicting facts, verifying availability and material details, documenting the contact, comparing qualified candidates, and routing unresolved verification to the correct next stage.

## Module 05 Operating Spine

Use this sequence throughout the module:

**PROFILE THE TRUCK → BUILD THE SEARCH → TRIAGE RESULTS → READ THE POSTING → CLASSIFY GAPS → SCREEN FIT → VERIFY AVAILABILITY/DETAILS → RECORD THE CONTACT → COMPARE CANDIDATES → REVIEW WRITTEN TERMS → HAND OFF UNRESOLVED RISK**

Real dispatch desks may overlap steps, but the sequence prevents a beginner from jumping from "I found a load" directly to "book it."

# 2. Where Freight Comes From

Freight opportunities reach a dispatcher through several channels, and no single channel is always superior:

* Commercial/public load boards — digital marketplaces open to registered users.
* Broker portals — a specific broker's own posting/booking system.
* Private load boards — access limited to a defined group (e.g., a broker's vetted carrier network).
* Broker-direct relationships — freight offered directly by a broker contact, outside a board.
* Shipper-direct opportunities — freight sourced directly from the party offering it, subject to the Module 02 carrier-side agency/broker boundary.
* Dedicated and contract freight — recurring or agreement-based freight (see Section 3).
* Spot-market freight — freight priced and tendered load-by-load.
* Email load lists and digital freight-matching platforms — freight distributed through automated matching or subscriber lists.

Most working dispatchers use several of these channels together, not just one.

# 3. Spot vs. Contract / Dedicated Freight

**FACT-M05-002.** These three categories describe how freight is priced and committed, not how urgent or valuable it is.

* Spot freight — commonly priced and tendered outside a longer-term contracted pricing arrangement, often load by load.
* Contract freight — freight moving under an established commercial agreement between the parties.
* Dedicated freight — recurring freight or capacity assigned to a specific carrier or service arrangement, often the most predictable of the three.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "spot always means emergency freight" — spot freight is simply priced outside a contract; it isn't inherently urgent. Do not teach "contract always means fixed rate" either — contract terms vary and can include rate structures other than a flat fixed rate.

# 4. What a Load Board Is

**FACT-M05-001.** A commercial load board is a digital freight-sourcing or freight-matching environment where users can locate available freight or available capacity.

A standard posting is a sourcing lead — a starting point for investigation, not a finished transaction.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "every posting is legally an offer to arrange" or "every posting is a binding contract." Some platforms include booking functionality governed by that platform's own separate terms — treat any such booking mechanic as [PLATFORM-SPECIFIC] and [CONTRACT-SPECIFIC], not as a general rule about what a posting legally means.

# 6. Build the Search From the Carrier Profile

Build every search from the carrier profile established in Module 04, not from general curiosity. Before opening a load board, answer: **What truck am I trying to move, from where, when, under what constraints?**

Translate the profile into three types of search information:

**Hard constraints** — a candidate cannot proceed if it conflicts with these. Examples can include equipment incompatibility, a required endorsement the driver does not hold, an explicit carrier prohibition, or a driver who is not available for the required pickup.

**Carrier/driver preferences** — important operating choices that shape the search but are not automatically laws. Examples can include preferred lanes, home-time goals, a carrier-set deadhead limit, preferred appointment patterns, or a preference to avoid a region.

**Verification questions** — information the posting does not establish well enough to decide. Examples can include exact facility location, commodity, dimensions, appointment time, facility hours, loading method, special instructions, or whether the load is still available.

## Worked Search-Profile Translation

Assume this fictional dispatch profile:

* Truck: 53-foot dry van
* Empty location: Fort Worth, Texas
* Available: Tuesday at 8:00 a.m.
* Driver: solo
* Carrier preference: Texas/Oklahoma/Arkansas/Louisiana outbound freight preferred
* Carrier restriction: no Northeast delivery
* Carrier-set origin deadhead target: preferably within 100 miles; owner approval required beyond that target
* Home-time commitment: driver needs to be back in the Dallas–Fort Worth area by Saturday evening
* Hazmat: driver does not hold the endorsement required for placarded hazmat
* Temperature-controlled capability: none

A disciplined dispatcher does not simply search "Texas loads." The profile becomes the search plan:

1. Start from the truck's actual empty location and availability time.
2. Select compatible dry-van freight.
3. Set an origin search area consistent with the carrier's deadhead preference, while recognizing that a wider search may be possible if the owner authorizes it.
4. Favor destination regions consistent with the carrier's lane preferences and home-time objective.
5. Exclude obvious prohibited destinations and incompatible freight.
6. Treat missing commodity, timing, facility, equipment or special-handling information as verification questions rather than favorable assumptions.
7. Keep a promising candidate only if its known facts survive the fit screen.

This is the difference between using filters and **building a search strategy**.

# 7. Origin, Destination & Radius

**IMPORTANT:** There is no universal search radius. Radius is a function of carrier policy, current market conditions, driver availability, and the carrier's broader repositioning plan — not a fixed number that applies to every carrier or situation. Any specific mileage figure used in this module's examples is an educational scenario, not a rule to memorize.

A dispatcher should also know whether a radius is a hard carrier limit, a normal target, or merely the current search starting point. If the carrier says "prefer under 100 miles but call me if the opportunity is unusually strong," a 105-mile candidate is not the same thing as violating a firm "do not exceed 100 miles" instruction. Record the actual instruction instead of silently converting a preference into a prohibition.

# 8. Deadhead

**FACT-M05-007.** Deadhead means empty movement — running without freight.

Origin deadhead is empty movement to the pickup location. This is the primary sourcing variable this module focuses on, because it directly affects whether a given load candidate is even worth pursuing for a specific truck's current position.

Destination-side (or "back-side") deadhead is different — empty repositioning after delivery, which may involve movement toward another load, another market, home, a terminal, maintenance, or parking.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not reduce destination deadhead to simply "driving home." Also, this module does not teach profitability math around deadhead cost; that belongs to Module 07's rate mathematics.

# 9. Reading a Load Posting

**FACT-M05-003.** Posted geography may not contain every routing or facility detail. Before trip planning or booking decisions, verify the exact pickup and delivery locations or ZIP codes.

Locate and interpret these common fields on a typical posting: origin, destination, pickup date, pickup window, delivery date, delivery window, appointment or FCFS status, equipment type, trailer requirement, commodity, weight, dimensions, load length, full/partial, stop count, posted rate if present, broker/company name, contact information, load reference number, posting age, hazmat indicator, temperature requirement, team requirement, driver-assist notes, special handling notes, and tarp/accessory requirements.

For posting-field review, classify material facts as:

* **KNOWN** — the posting states the fact clearly enough for this stage.
* **MISSING** — the field or required fact is absent.
* **UNCLEAR** — information is present but ambiguous, incomplete or internally inconsistent.
* **VERIFY** — the fact may be shown, but the dispatcher must confirm it before relying on it for the next decision.

Across the wider Academy, **UNKNOWN** is the umbrella condition for information you cannot safely rely on yet. In Module 05, MISSING and UNCLEAR explain *why* a field is unknown; VERIFY states the required action. These labels are complementary, not competing systems.

## Worked Imperfect Posting

Fictional posting:

> Dallas, TX → Memphis, TN | Tue pickup | Wed delivery | VAN | 39,500 lb | "consumer goods" | FCFS | $1,450 | Load DV-4821 | Notes: "driver assist possible; call for details"

Do not read this as a complete load. Annotate it:

* Origin: Dallas — **KNOWN at city level; VERIFY exact facility/address before later trip planning.**
* Destination: Memphis — **KNOWN at city level; VERIFY exact facility/address.**
* Pickup: Tuesday — **UNCLEAR.** No operating hours or pickup window shown.
* Delivery: Wednesday — **UNCLEAR.** No operating hours/window shown.
* Equipment: VAN — **KNOWN at posting level**, but confirm any special trailer requirement if the call reveals one.
* Weight: 39,500 lb — **KNOWN at posting level**, subject to the equipment/weight fit checks learned earlier.
* Commodity: consumer goods — **KNOWN only at a broad level.** Ask whether any handling, temperature, hazmat or other material requirement applies.
* FCFS: **KNOWN as service-order type; facility hours MISSING.** FCFS does not mean 24-hour access.
* Rate: $1,450 — **KNOWN as posted rate**, not yet proof of final negotiated/written terms.
* Driver assist: "possible" — **UNCLEAR / VERIFY.** What task, at which stop, and under what conditions?
* Availability: **VERIFY.** Visibility on the board is not confirmation the load remains open.

Convert the gaps into call questions. A professional dispatcher now has a reason for every question rather than reading a generic script.

# 10. Pickup / Delivery Windows

A pickup or delivery window is a range of acceptable times, not a single guaranteed moment. Confirm whether a posting shows a specific date, a date range, or a vague placeholder — and treat vague timing as a gap to verify, not something to assume favorably or unfavorably.

# 11. Appointment vs. FCFS

"Appointment" means a specific scheduled time has been arranged with the facility. "FCFS" (first come, first served) means arrival order determines service order, without a locked appointment time.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not equate FCFS with 24-hour facility access. FCFS still operates within the facility's actual operating hours — a posting listing FCFS without stated facility hours is missing a real piece of operational information.

# 12. Equipment / Freight Fit Screen

**FACT-M05-006.** Before pursuing any candidate, run the fit screen using what you already know from Modules 03 and 04: equipment type, weight, dimensions, loading method, carrier restrictions, lane restrictions, driver availability, home time, hazmat, temperature, team/solo status, and origin deadhead.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not pitch a load to a carrier or driver that obviously fails this screen. Screening out clear bad fits before they reach a human being is a core time-saving skill.

# 13. Carrier / Driver Profile Screen

Every candidate needs to be checked specifically against the profile you built in Module 04 — not against a generic idea of "good freight." A load that would be an excellent fit for one carrier can be a clear rejection for another, purely based on that carrier's own restrictions and preferences.

# 14. Missing Information

**FACT-M05-008.** Required workflow when a posting is incomplete:

**DO NOT ASSUME → IDENTIFY THE GAP → CONVERT THE GAP INTO A QUESTION → VERIFY → DOCUMENT THE ANSWER/SOURCE → CONTINUE, HOLD OR REJECT**

Common categories include exact origin, exact destination, commodity, weight, dimensions, stop count, loading/unloading method, pickup/delivery hours, appointment/FCFS details, temperature, hazmat, special handling, equipment requirements, team/solo status, accessorial expectations and availability.

## Search-Result Triage Before You Call

A working dispatcher may see many results at once. Do not give every result equal attention. Triage them into four operating buckets:

* **REJECT NOW** — a known fact creates an obvious equipment, qualification, carrier-policy or availability conflict.
* **INSPECT NOW** — the known facts make the candidate promising enough for immediate posting review/contact.
* **VERIFY** — potentially usable, but a material missing/unclear fact prevents a safe decision.
* **HOLD / LOWER PRIORITY** — not disqualified, but weaker than current alternatives or dependent on a later decision.

Posting age can affect priority, but age alone is not a universal reject rule. Record why a candidate moved into a bucket so another dispatcher or instructor can reconstruct the decision.

## Evidence Standard for the Sourcing Desk

For every candidate you actively pursue, preserve enough information to answer:

* Where did we find it?
* When did we review/contact it?
* What load/reference number identified it?
* Which material facts were known?
* Which were missing, unclear or required verification?
* Who supplied the clarification?
* What decision did we make and why?
* If unresolved, where did we hand it off next?

A dispatcher who cannot reconstruct why a load was advanced, held or rejected is relying on memory instead of a professional process.
