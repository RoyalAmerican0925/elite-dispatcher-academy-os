Student Manual — Module 05

Load Boards & Freight Sourcing

Assignment C-005

# 1. Module Introduction

You now know the industry, your legal boundaries, your equipment, and how to onboard a carrier and driver properly. This module answers the next practical question: where do you actually find freight, how do you read a posting correctly, and how do you decide — quickly — whether a load is worth pursuing for this specific carrier, driver, and equipment?

IMPORTANT: Core principle for this entire module: A LOAD POSTING IS A LEAD. It does not automatically prove the freight is still available, that all details are complete, that the carrier is qualified for it, that the broker is legitimate, that the rate is acceptable, or that the load is booked. Every posting starts as a lead and has to earn its way through several stages before it becomes a booked load.

The professional skill this module builds isn't "call every load." It's quickly eliminating obvious bad fits, identifying missing facts, verifying availability, and passing qualified opportunities into the next decision stage — broker screening, pricing, negotiation, booking, trip planning, or HOS review.

# 2. Where Freight Comes From

Freight opportunities reach a dispatcher through several channels, and no single channel is always superior:

Commercial/public load boards — digital marketplaces open to registered users.

Broker portals — a specific broker's own posting/booking system.

Private load boards — access limited to a defined group (e.g., a broker's vetted carrier network).

Broker-direct relationships — freight offered directly by a broker contact, outside a board.

Shipper-direct opportunities — freight sourced directly from the party that owns it (subject to the Module 02 boundary — see Section 19).

Dedicated and contract freight — recurring or agreement-based freight (see Section 3).

Spot-market freight — freight priced and tendered load-by-load.

Email load lists and digital freight-matching platforms — freight distributed through automated matching or subscriber lists.

Most working dispatchers use several of these channels together, not just one.

# 3. Spot vs. Contract / Dedicated Freight

FACT-M05-002. These three categories describe how freight is priced and committed, not how urgent or valuable it is.

Spot freight — commonly priced and tendered outside a longer-term contracted pricing arrangement, often load by load.

Contract freight — freight moving under an established commercial agreement between the parties.

Dedicated freight — recurring freight or capacity assigned to a specific carrier or service arrangement, often the most predictable of the three.

IMPORTANT: Do not teach "spot always means emergency freight" — spot freight is simply priced outside a contract; it isn't inherently urgent. Do not teach "contract always means fixed rate" either — contract terms vary and can include rate structures other than a flat fixed rate.

# 4. What a Load Board Is

FACT-M05-001. A commercial load board is a digital freight-sourcing or freight-matching environment where users can locate available freight or available capacity.

A standard posting is a sourcing lead — a starting point for investigation, not a finished transaction.

IMPORTANT: Do not teach "every posting is legally an offer to arrange" or "every posting is a binding contract." Some platforms include booking functionality governed by that platform's own separate terms — treat any such booking mechanic as [PLATFORM-SPECIFIC] and [CONTRACT-SPECIFIC], not as a general rule about what a posting legally means.

# 6. Building a Search

Build every search from the carrier profile you established in Module 04, not from general curiosity. Common search parameters include: truck empty location, origin radius, destination or destination region, pickup date, equipment type, full vs. partial, weight, length, carrier lane preferences, restricted lanes, home-time requirements, driver availability, hazmat capability, temperature capability, and team/solo status.

# 7. Origin, Destination & Radius

IMPORTANT: There is no universal search radius. Radius is a function of carrier policy, current market conditions, driver availability, and the carrier's broader repositioning plan — not a fixed number that applies to every carrier or situation. Any specific mileage figure used in this module's examples is an educational scenario, not a rule to memorize.

# 8. Deadhead

FACT-M05-007. Deadhead means empty movement — running without freight.

Origin deadhead is empty movement to the pickup location. This is the primary sourcing variable this module focuses on, because it directly affects whether a given load candidate is even worth pursuing for a specific truck's current position.

Destination-side (or "back-side") deadhead is different — empty repositioning after delivery, which may involve movement toward another load, another market, home, a terminal, maintenance, or parking.

IMPORTANT: Do not reduce destination deadhead to simply "driving home" — it can serve several different operational purposes. Also, this module does not teach profitability math around deadhead cost; that belongs to Module 07's rate mathematics.

# 9. Reading a Load Posting

FACT-M05-003. Posted geography may not contain every routing or facility detail. Before trip planning or booking decisions, verify the exact pickup and delivery locations or ZIP codes. Then locate and interpret these common fields on a typical posting: origin, destination, pickup date, pickup window, delivery date, delivery window, appointment or FCFS status, equipment type, trailer requirement, commodity, weight, dimensions, load length, full/partial, stop count, posted rate if present, broker/company name, contact information, load reference number, posting age, hazmat indicator, temperature requirement, team requirement, driver-assist notes, special handling notes, and tarp/accessory requirements.

For every field, classify it as one of: KNOWN, MISSING, UNCLEAR, or VERIFY. This classification habit is what turns a load posting from something you passively read into something you actively evaluate.

# 10. Pickup / Delivery Windows

A pickup or delivery window is a range of acceptable times, not a single guaranteed moment. Confirm whether a posting shows a specific date, a date range, or a vague placeholder — and treat vague timing as a gap to verify, not something to assume favorably or unfavorably.

# 11. Appointment vs. FCFS

"Appointment" means a specific scheduled time has been arranged with the facility. "FCFS" (first come, first served) means arrival order determines service order, without a locked appointment time.

IMPORTANT: Do not equate FCFS with 24-hour facility access. FCFS still operates within the facility's actual operating hours — a posting listing FCFS without stated facility hours is missing a real piece of operational information, not a sign that the facility is always open. See Scenario S-05-05.

# 12. Equipment / Freight Fit Screen

FACT-M05-006. Before pursuing any candidate, run the fit screen using what you already know from Modules 03 and 04: equipment type, weight, dimensions, loading method, carrier restrictions, lane restrictions, driver availability, home time, hazmat, temperature, team/solo status, and origin deadhead.

IMPORTANT: Do not pitch a load to a carrier or driver that obviously fails this screen. Screening out clear bad fits before they reach a human being is the core time-saving skill this module builds.

# 13. Carrier / Driver Profile Screen

Every candidate needs to be checked specifically against the profile you built in Module 04 — not against a generic idea of "good freight." A load that would be an excellent fit for one carrier can be a clear rejection for another, purely based on that carrier's own restrictions and preferences. See Scenarios S-05-02 and S-05-03.

# 14. Missing Information

FACT-M05-008. Required workflow when a posting is incomplete: do not assume → identify the gap → verify → document → continue or reject.

Common categories of missing information: exact origin, exact destination, commodity, weight, dimensions, stop count, loading method, unloading method, pickup hours, delivery hours, appointment/FCFS details, temperature, hazmat, special handling, equipment requirements, team/solo status, and accessorial expectations.

IMPORTANT: Do not make students (or, later, yourself on the job) guess at missing information to keep a deal moving. A gap gets verified or the load gets set aside — it doesn't get filled in with an assumption.

# 15. Posting Age

FACT-M05-004. Posting age is a priority signal — it helps you decide what to look at first. It is NOT proof that a load is available or unavailable.

IMPORTANT: Do not teach universal age thresholds like "under 15 minutes is good" or "14 hours is stale" or "24 hours means unavailable." Platform refresh and removal behavior varies by platform and by poster — a load's actual availability always has to be verified directly, regardless of how old or fresh the posting looks. See Scenario S-05-06.

# 16. Duplicate / Reposted Freight

FACT-M05-005. Similar-looking listings from multiple sources can have many different explanations.

Separate legitimate freight that simply looks similar.

Authorized co-brokering between two legitimate parties.

A shipper tendering the same freight through multiple channels at once.

A reposted or duplicated platform record with no ill intent.

Unauthorized brokerage or fraud — a real possibility, but only one of several.

IMPORTANT: Do not diagnose double brokering just because two postings look alike. This module's response to a duplicate-looking posting is: flag it, verify the basic details, and route it to Module 06 for fraud screening if something remains genuinely unresolved — not to jump straight to a fraud conclusion here. See Scenario S-05-07.

# 17. Availability Verification

FACT-M05-009. FOUND does not equal AVAILABLE.

A posting must be confirmed through the posting party or the applicable platform booking process before it's treated as currently available. Do not assume a load is still open simply because the posting remains visible — see Scenario S-05-12, where a promising-looking load turns out to already be covered.
