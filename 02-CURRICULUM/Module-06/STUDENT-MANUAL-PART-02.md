Callback verification means confirming a change or request by calling a previously trusted number — not the number provided in the message that raised the concern. This habit is a strong operational defense against BEC and payment-redirection fraud, and it should become automatic for any change to payment, routing, or contact information.

# 12\. Document Cross-Checking

Compare across every document you have for a load: broker legal name, MC/identifying number, address, contact information, load number, carrier name, pickup, delivery, rate, special terms, and payment instructions.

**IMPORTANT:** An inconsistency between documents means VERIFY, not automatically FRAUD. Document appearance alone — a professional-looking logo, formatting, letterhead — is not sufficient proof of legitimacy either way.

# 13\. Rate Confirmation Mismatches

A rate confirmation with a name, MC number, or payment instruction that doesn't match your other records on file for this broker is a red flag requiring resolution before signing — not proof of fraud on its own.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not sign a rate confirmation with an unresolved mismatch, and do not call it fraud from the document alone. Resolve it through trusted-channel verification first.

# 14\. Co-Brokering vs. Double Brokering

**FACT-M06-006.** These terms are not synonyms.

Authorized co-brokering can legitimately exist — two brokers working together with appropriate knowledge and authorization. "Double brokering" is commonly used in the industry for unauthorized re-brokering, including situations where a motor carrier accepts freight and transfers it to another carrier without the original broker's or shipper's knowledge.

**[LEGAL / INDUSTRY TERMINOLOGY — QUALIFIED]** Do not diagnose double brokering from duplicate listings alone. A student's task when something looks duplicated is to identify the inconsistency, ask questions, verify authority, verify relationships where appropriate, and escalate unresolved issues.

# 15\. Credit / Factor Indicators

**FACT-M06-007.** A factoring company decline is a financial-risk decision by that factor — it is not, by itself, proof of fraud, criminality, or invalid authority.

A factor approval can support operational payment planning. A factor decline can mean an exposure limit was reached, the broker is a slow payer, there's a credit issue, it's an internal factor policy choice, or some other financial concern entirely unrelated to legitimacy.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not accuse a broker of fraud because a factor declined them. If carrier policy requires factor approval before booking, a decline can still mean DO NOT USE under that policy — but that's a policy outcome, not a fraud finding.

# 16\. DNU (Do-Not-Use) Lists

Do-Not-Use lists are carrier policy and internal risk control — not a government blacklist. Reasons a broker might land on a DNU list include payment history, service disputes, fraud concern, identity mismatch, owner preference, or factoring restrictions.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not describe DNU lists as official government determinations. They reflect one company's internal risk decisions.

# 17\. Payment-Path Verification

Building on Module 04's payment-change protocol: new bank, factoring, or remittance instructions are never trusted from the message alone. Use a previously verified contact method, callback-verify, compare entity/account/factor information against what's on file, document the verification, and update only after confirmation.

**IMPORTANT:** This applies with equal force whether the change arrives by email, text, phone call, or an in-person conversation with someone claiming new authority.

# 18\. Common Red Flags — Summary Reference

None of these prove fraud individually. Each one triggers VERIFY, not an automatic conclusion:

* Contact channel doesn't match records on file.
* Email domain has subtle irregularities.
* Unexpected payment/routing/factoring change.
* Document details don't match across sources.
* Required broker authority shows inactive or lapsed.
* Financial security shows a pending cancellation or trustee issue.
* Similar freight posted under multiple company names.
* Urgent pressure to skip verification steps.
* Multiple small inconsistencies accumulating across a single transaction.

# 19\. Myth Audit

### Myth 1: "A real MC number means it's a safe broker."

**Correct principle:** Entity registration is one verification layer, not the whole picture. It doesn't verify the contact person, the payment path, or whether this specific transaction is legitimate.

### Myth 2: "A normal-looking email address means it's a safe message."

**Correct principle:** A legitimate account can be compromised. Appearance doesn't rule out account takeover or thread hijacking.

### Myth 3: "Low credit automatically means fraud."

**Correct principle:** Credit and financial risk are separate from fraud.

### Myth 4: "A factor decline automatically means fraud."

**Correct principle:** A decline reflects that factor's own risk decision — exposure limits, slow pay, internal policy — not a fraud finding.

### Myth 5: "A duplicate posting automatically means double brokering."

**Correct principle:** Duplicate listings have several possible innocent explanations. Verify before concluding anything.

### Myth 6: "Co-brokering and double brokering are the same thing."

**Correct principle:** Co-brokering can be authorized and legitimate; double brokering specifically refers to unauthorized re-brokering.

### Myth 7: "A professional-looking logo means it's a legitimate document."

**Correct principle:** Document appearance alone doesn't verify legitimacy — logos and formatting can be copied easily.

### Myth 8: "A SAFER company record verifies the individual contacting you."

**Correct principle:** Entity verification and contact verification are separate questions. SAFER answers the first, not the second.
