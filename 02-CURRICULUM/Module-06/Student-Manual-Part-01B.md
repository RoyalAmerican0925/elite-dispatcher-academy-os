**FACT-M06-005.** A legitimate email account can be compromised. Unexpected changes involving payment, factoring, routing, pickup/delivery, bank information, new attachments, or urgent requests must trigger independent verification through a trusted channel — regardless of how legitimate the account otherwise looks.

Required rule: STOP → do not click or change anything → use a trusted channel → verify → document → escalate if needed.

**IMPORTANT:** This module does not teach email-header forensics, IP tracing, or hacking investigation — that's specialized cybersecurity work outside dispatcher instruction. Your job is to recognize the pattern and route it correctly, not to investigate the compromise yourself.

# 11\. Callback Verification

Callback verification means confirming a change or request by calling a previously trusted number — not the number provided in the message that raised the concern. This habit is a strong operational defense against BEC and payment-redirection fraud, and it should become automatic for any change to payment, routing, or contact information.

# 12\. Document Cross-Checking

Compare across every document you have for a load: broker legal name, MC/identifying number, address, contact information, load number, carrier name, pickup, delivery, rate, special terms, and payment instructions.

**IMPORTANT:** An inconsistency between documents means VERIFY, not automatically FRAUD. Document appearance alone — a professional-looking logo, formatting, letterhead — is not sufficient proof of legitimacy either way. See Section 13 and Myth 7 in Section 20.

# 13\. Rate Confirmation Mismatches

A rate confirmation with a name, MC number, or payment instruction that doesn't match your other records on file for this broker is a red flag requiring resolution before signing — not proof of fraud on its own.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not sign a rate confirmation with an unresolved mismatch, and do not call it fraud from the document alone. Resolve it through trusted-channel verification first. See Scenario S-06-05.

# 14\. Co-Brokering vs. Double Brokering

**FACT-M06-006.** These terms are not synonyms.

Authorized co-brokering can legitimately exist — two brokers working together with appropriate knowledge and authorization. "Double brokering" is commonly used in the industry for unauthorized re-brokering, including situations where a motor carrier accepts freight and transfers it to another carrier without the original broker's or shipper's knowledge.

**[LEGAL / INDUSTRY TERMINOLOGY — QUALIFIED]** Do not diagnose double brokering from duplicate listings alone. A student's task when something looks duplicated is to identify the inconsistency, ask questions, verify authority, verify relationships where appropriate, and escalate unresolved issues — not to independently determine complex contractual legality. See Scenario S-06-06.

# 15\. Credit / Factor Indicators

**FACT-M06-007.** A factoring company decline is a financial-risk decision by that factor — it is not, by itself, proof of fraud, criminality, or invalid authority.

A factor approval can support operational payment planning. A factor decline can mean an exposure limit was reached, the broker is a slow payer, there's a credit issue, it's an internal factor policy choice, or some other financial concern entirely unrelated to legitimacy.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not accuse a broker of fraud because a factor declined them. If carrier policy requires factor approval before booking, a decline can still mean DO NOT USE under that policy — but that's a policy outcome, not a fraud finding. See Scenario S-06-07.

# 16\. DNU (Do-Not-Use) Lists

Do-Not-Use lists are carrier policy and internal risk control — not a government blacklist. Reasons a broker might land on a DNU list include payment history, service disputes, fraud concern, identity mismatch, owner preference, or factoring restrictions.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not describe DNU lists as official government determinations. They reflect one company's internal risk decisions, which may be based on very different reasons from company to company.
