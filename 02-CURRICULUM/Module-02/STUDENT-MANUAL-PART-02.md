**IMPORTANT:** There is no numerical safe harbor. Do not treat any specific carrier count — one, three, or any other number — as an automatic legal limit. The number of carriers represented is not, by itself, the test.

# 11\. Non-Overlapping vs. Overlapping Carrier Operations

### A. Non-overlapping operations

FMCSA's guidance gives helpful examples: a dispatch service representing a reefer (refrigerated) carrier and a flatbed carrier, where a given load can only physically move on one type of equipment, has little or no discretion to allocate that load between the two — only one of them could haul it in the first place. The same logic applies to carriers operating in genuinely separate, non-overlapping geographic territories. When the same piece of freight could not reasonably go to either carrier, the opportunity for allocation discretion is reduced or eliminated.

### B. Overlapping operations

If multiple represented carriers could all reasonably haul the same load — for example, three dry-van carriers all capable of running the same lane — and the dispatch service is the one deciding which of them gets it, that decision-making is the allocation-of-traffic activity discussed in Section 9. This is the fact pattern that raises the most brokerage risk.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 12\. Load Boards and Carrier-Side Sourcing

Sourcing a load from a load board on behalf of a carrier you have an ongoing relationship with — where you're finding freight for that carrier's trucks, not deciding among several carriers who gets it — is consistent with the carrier-side agency model this course teaches. The load board itself is just a tool; what matters is who you're acting for and what decision-making authority you're exercising.

# 13\. Shipper Contact and Solicitation

Contacting shippers to say, in substance, "I can arrange transportation for your freight using my network of carriers" looks very different from sourcing loads on behalf of one specific carrier you represent. Offering to arrange transportation generally — across a network, without a specific represented carrier already identified — is exactly the kind of activity the broker definition in Section 5 describes.

**IMPORTANT:** This is a common way well-intentioned dispatch services drift into broker territory: general shipper solicitation, phrased as a service offering, can itself satisfy the "offers to arrange transportation" language in the broker definition — even before any load is actually booked.

# 14\. Compensation Structures

**FACT-M02-005.** No single fee structure — flat fee, percentage of the load, or per-truck fee — automatically determines broker status by itself. FMCSA's guidance treats the source of compensation as one relevant factor among several, not a standalone test.

Payment from the represented motor carrier, under the dispatch relationship, supports carrier-agent treatment. Payment from a broker, a shipper, a factoring company, or otherwise drawn from the transportation transaction's funds may weigh toward brokerage — but only as one factor considered alongside the others in this module, not as an automatic conclusion on its own.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "percentage compensation automatically equals brokerage." It doesn't. It's one fact among several that gets weighed together.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 15\. Freight-Money Flow

Handling the money that moves between a shipper and a motor carrier is, according to FMCSA's guidance, a factor that strongly suggests the need for broker authority. But FMCSA has been explicit that handling money is not, by itself, an essential requirement for broker status — it's a strong indicator, not an automatic single-factor test.

Example fact pattern to reason through, not a rule to memorize: a dispatch service receives the freight charge directly from the shipper, keeps its fee, and forwards the remainder to the carrier. That's a strong brokerage-risk pattern — but even here, the full picture (the carrier relationship, the allocation question, the agreement) still matters to the complete analysis.

**IMPORTANT:** Never declare a legal conclusion from one fact in isolation — money flow included. Weigh it alongside the other factors this module covers.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 16\. Written Agreements: What They Can and Cannot Do

A properly structured written agreement between a dispatch service and a carrier can document the agency relationship, responsibilities, compensation, authority, limits, and the continuing nature of the relationship. This is valuable — and, per Section 8, it's a factor FMCSA's guidance treats as supporting bona fide agent status.

**IMPORTANT:** A contract's title does not control actual conduct. A document titled "Bona Fide Agent Agreement" does not make allocation-of-traffic activity lawful if what actually happens day to day meets the broker definition. Regulators and courts look at conduct, not just paperwork labels.

# 17\. The 1099 / W-2 Myth

Tax classification does not independently determine broker status. FMCSA's guidance recognizes that a bona fide agent may be structured as either an employee or an independent contractor of the carrier. Being paid on a 1099 doesn't make someone a dispatcher any more than being on a W-2 does — the transportation activity and the carrier relationship are what the analysis actually turns on.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "1099 means dispatcher" or "W-2 is required to be a dispatcher." Neither is accurate.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 18\. Introduction to Formal Broker Authority

**FACT-M02-006.** For introductory context only — this is not a broker-licensing course. Property brokers must generally satisfy FMCSA registration requirements, which as of 2026 include broker operating authority registration, designation of a process agent using Form BOC-3, and financial security of $75,000 using either a surety bond (Form BMC-84) or a qualifying trust fund (Form BMC-85).

CURRENT 2026 RULE — VERIFIED AUGUST 11, 2026: Effective January 16, 2026, FMCSA updated the eligibility requirements for entities that may serve as BMC-85 trust providers. If FMCSA determines that a trust provider is ineligible, an affected broker or freight forwarder generally has 30 days to obtain a replacement filing from a qualified provider or face suspension of operating authority. Separately, when available financial security falls below $75,000, the broker or freight forwarder generally has seven business days after FMCSA notice to replenish the security before suspension procedures apply.

The purpose of this section is narrow: to show students that broker status is a distinct, separately regulated role with its own registration and financial-security obligations — not to teach the mechanics of becoming a broker.

Source classification: REGULATION (49 CFR § 387.307) — current requirements effective January 16, 2026; verified August 11, 2026.

# 19\. Unauthorized Brokerage: Consequences

**FACT-M02-007.** Federal law (49 U.S.C. § 14916) establishes a civil-penalty framework for unauthorized brokerage activity. A person who knowingly authorizes, consents to, or permits unauthorized brokerage is liable to the United States government for a civil penalty, and separately liable to any injured party for valid claims, without a cap tied to that second category.

This liability applies jointly and severally to the corporate entity or partnership involved, and individually to the officers, directors, and principals of that entity.

CURRENT 2026 PENALTY — VERIFIED AUGUST 11, 2026: The statute states a maximum civil penalty of $10,000 per knowing violation, but the inflation-adjusted maximum currently codified for 2026 is $13,647 per violation under 49 CFR § 1022.4. A January 2026 adjustment initially listed $14,020, but the Surface Transportation Board revised the 2026 table effective May 5, 2026; the current 2026 amount is $13,647. Because civil monetary penalty schedules can be revised, verify the current table again at the academy's final publication/release gate.

Two things this section deliberately does not do: it does not use the phrase "piercing the corporate veil" (that phrase does not appear in the statute's text, even though some commentary uses it informally), and it does not tell students that specific personal assets — a house, a car, a bank account — will necessarily be seized. The statute establishes a liability framework; it does not itself describe asset seizure mechanics, which depend on separate collection proceedings.

*Source classification: STATUTE (49 U.S.C. § 14916)*
