**Why someone may believe it:** The carrier-side dispatcher role is built around agency for a carrier, so it's easy to assume the broker rules simply don't apply to dispatchers at all.

**Correct principle:** Whether broker authority is needed depends on the actual conduct and relationship — not on the dispatcher label. A dispatch service that drifts into arranging transportation generally, or allocating traffic between carriers, can trigger broker requirements regardless of its title.

**Why it matters:** Assuming blanket immunity from broker rules is exactly the mindset that leads dispatch services into unauthorized brokerage without realizing it.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 2: "A dispatcher may only represent one carrier."

**Why someone may believe it:** Some read the bona fide agent regulation narrowly and conclude a single-carrier limit must be built in.

**Correct principle:** There is no numerical limit in the regulation or guidance. Representing multiple carriers is fine when it doesn't create allocation-of-traffic discretion — see Section 11.

**Why it matters:** Believing a hard one-carrier rule exists can cause a dispatcher to either needlessly limit their business, or, in the opposite direction, assume any multi-carrier setup is automatically fine as long as it's more than one — neither assumption is correct.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 3: "A dispatcher may legally represent up to three carriers."

**Why someone may believe it:** This specific number circulates informally in the industry, sometimes presented as an official safe harbor.

**Correct principle:** No such numerical safe harbor exists in the statute, regulation, or FMCSA guidance. The analysis is about allocation of traffic, not carrier count.

**Why it matters:** Relying on an invented number instead of the actual factors (overlap, discretion, relationship) creates false confidence in situations that may still carry real brokerage risk.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 4: "A dispatch agreement automatically protects you."

**Why someone may believe it:** A well-drafted written agreement feels like a legal shield, especially if it's labeled correctly.

**Correct principle:** Contract labels do not control actual conduct. An agreement titled "Bona Fide Agent Agreement" doesn't make allocation-of-traffic activity lawful if that's what's actually happening.

**Why it matters:** Treating paperwork as a substitute for correct conduct is one of the more dangerous myths in this list, because it creates a false sense of security.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 5: "A 1099 independent contractor is automatically a dispatcher."

**Why someone may believe it:** Tax status and job function get conflated in casual industry conversation.

**Correct principle:** FMCSA guidance recognizes a bona fide agent may be an employee or a contractor. Tax classification doesn't independently determine broker status.

**Why it matters:** This myth can distract from the factors that actually matter — the carrier relationship and the nature of the activity.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 6: "Getting paid a percentage automatically makes you a broker."

**Why someone may believe it:** Percentage-based fees resemble how some brokers are paid, so the fee structure alone feels disqualifying.

**Correct principle:** No single fee structure automatically determines broker status. Compensation source is one factor among several, not a standalone test.

**Why it matters:** This myth can cause a legitimately structured dispatch service to abandon a normal compensation model out of unnecessary fear.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 7: "If you never touch the freight, you cannot be a broker."

**Why someone may believe it:** "Brokers move money and paperwork, not trucks" gets flipped into "if I never touch anything physical, I'm not a broker."

**Correct principle:** Brokers by definition don't transport freight themselves — that's true of every broker. Not touching the freight is consistent with being a broker, not evidence against it.

**Why it matters:** This myth misunderstands the broker definition entirely — not transporting freight is the broker's normal condition, not a defense.

*Source classification: STATUTE / REGULATION*

### Myth 8: "Calling yourself a dispatch service prevents broker classification."

**Why someone may believe it:** If "dispatch service" isn't defined in federal law, some assume that means it's outside federal law's reach.

**Correct principle:** FMCSA guidance directly addresses this: dispatch-service activities may fall into broker or bona fide-agent treatment depending on the nature and scope of the activity — not the label used.

**Why it matters:** This is the myth this entire module works hardest to correct — activity and relationship control, not title.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 9: "A dispatcher can freely move a booked load from one client carrier to another."

**Why someone may believe it:** If a carrier can't perform, reassigning the load to another represented carrier feels like normal problem-solving.

**Correct principle:** Substituting one independent represented carrier for another after a booking is exactly the kind of decision-making that can constitute allocation of traffic — this materially changes the analysis compared to simply canceling and returning the opportunity. See Scenario 6 in Section 22.

**Why it matters:** This is a realistic, easy-to-miss scenario where good customer-service instincts can create real brokerage risk.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

# 21\. Decision Tool: STOP — IDENTIFY — CHECK — ESCALATE

Use this tool any time you're unsure whether a situation is straightforward carrier-side dispatching or something that might require broker authority.

### STOP

Do not assume a title — "dispatcher," "dispatch service," "bona fide agent" — makes an activity legal. Pause before proceeding.

### IDENTIFY

Who are you representing in this transaction? One carrier you have an ongoing relationship with, or are you acting more broadly?

### CHECK

* Who owns the freight opportunity — did it come to you through your represented carrier, or independently?

* Who selected the carrier for this load — you, exercising discretion among options, or was there really only one carrier who could take it?

* Who pays you, and from what source — the carrier, or funds moving through the transportation transaction itself?

* Are multiple represented carriers competing for the same load, with you deciding who gets it?

* Are you receiving and handling freight funds between a shipper and a carrier?

* Are you soliciting freight for one named, represented carrier — or offering to arrange transportation generally?

### ESCALATE

If the facts create real uncertainty about whether broker authority is required, stop before proceeding and get qualified regulatory or legal guidance rather than guessing.

**IMPORTANT:** This tool is educational issue-spotting to help you recognize when a situation needs a closer look. It is not individualized legal advice, and using it correctly does not guarantee any particular legal outcome.

# 22\. Scenario Lab

### Scenario 1: One-Carrier Load-Board Sourcing

*All names and companies are fictional.*

Dispatcher Renata has an ongoing agreement with Hallmark Freightways, a single for-hire carrier. She searches a load board daily for freight that fits Hallmark's trucks and books loads exclusively on Hallmark's behalf.

**Classification:** Consistent with carrier-side agency, subject to the complete relationship and conduct.

Renata represents one carrier under an ongoing relationship and isn't allocating traffic between competing carriers. This is the clearest example of low-risk carrier-side dispatching in this module — though even here, the full relationship (agreement, compensation source, conduct) is still what ultimately controls, not just the single-carrier fact alone.

### Scenario 2: General Shipper Solicitation

*All names and companies are fictional.*

A dispatch service sends shippers a message stating: "I can arrange transportation for your freight using my network of carriers."

**Classification:** Raises broker concerns.

This is an offer to arrange transportation generally, not sourcing for one specific represented carrier. It closely tracks the broker definition in Section 5 — "offers to arrange transportation... for compensation" — before any specific load is even booked.

### Scenario 3: Non-Overlapping Equipment

*All names and companies are fictional.*

A dispatch service represents Carrier A (reefer-equipped only) and Carrier B (flatbed-equipped only). A frozen-freight opportunity comes in that only reefer equipment can haul.

**Classification:** Lower allocation-of-traffic risk due to non-overlap.
