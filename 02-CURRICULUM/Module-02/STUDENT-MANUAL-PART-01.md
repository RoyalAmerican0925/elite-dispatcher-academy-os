**ELITE DISPATCHER ACADEMY**

**Student Manual — Module 02**

Dispatcher Role & Legal Boundaries

*Assignment C-002*

# 1\. Module Introduction

Module 01 answered who exists in this industry and how they connect. This module answers a sharper question: what exactly does a carrier-side dispatcher do, what authority does that role carry, and where can dispatch activity cross into freight brokerage — a separate, federally regulated business that requires its own registration?

This is one of the highest-stakes topics in this academy. Get it wrong in the real world, and the consequences aren't just a bad grade — they can include federal civil penalties and personal liability. The goal here isn't to scare you away from dispatching. It's to give you the judgment to recognize where the boundary sits, so you can operate confidently and legally on the correct side of it.

**IMPORTANT:** Nothing in this chapter is individualized legal advice. It teaches you to recognize regulatory issues so you know when to slow down and get qualified guidance — it does not replace that guidance.

# 2\. What a Carrier-Side Dispatcher Actually Does

A carrier-side dispatcher works on behalf of a motor carrier — coordinating that carrier's trucks, drivers, and freight. Day to day, this typically includes finding and evaluating load opportunities for the carrier's trucks, communicating with brokers or shippers about those opportunities, assigning drivers and equipment, planning routes and schedules, tracking loads in progress, and solving problems as they arise.

**Dispatcher application:** Everything in this list is done in service of the carrier that employs or contracts the dispatcher. The dispatcher's job is to make that one carrier's operation run well — not to run a marketplace connecting many different carriers to many different shippers.

# 3\. Dispatcher Responsibilities vs. Carrier Responsibilities

The dispatcher and the motor carrier are connected but distinct. The carrier holds the operating authority, owns or contracts the equipment, employs or contracts the drivers, and bears ultimate legal responsibility for the transportation. The dispatcher acts on the carrier's behalf, under the carrier's direction, to coordinate that transportation.

**IMPORTANT:** This distinction matters because a dispatcher's authority to act comes from the carrier relationship. A dispatcher isn't an independent business making its own transportation decisions in the open market — at least not while remaining inside the carrier-side agency model this course teaches.

# 4\. Why Legal Boundaries Matter

Freight brokerage is a separately regulated federal activity. Someone who arranges transportation by an authorized motor carrier, for compensation, may be acting as a broker — and brokers must register with FMCSA, post financial security, and meet other requirements. A dispatcher who crosses into broker-type conduct without broker authority isn't just breaking a technicality. Federal law creates real civil liability for unauthorized brokerage, discussed fully in Section 19\.

**Dispatcher application:** Knowing where dispatching ends and brokering begins protects you, the carriers you work with, and the business you're building. This isn't a one-time test you pass and forget — it's a judgment you'll apply to real situations throughout your career.

# 5\. Broker Definition — Beginner Version

**FACT-M02-001.** Under federal law, a broker is a person — other than a motor carrier, or a qualifying employee or bona fide agent of a motor carrier — who, for compensation, arranges or offers to arrange the transportation of property by an authorized motor carrier.

This definition comes from statute (49 U.S.C. § 13102\) and is restated in federal regulation (49 CFR § 371.2(a)).

### The core distinction

* Carrier \= transports the freight.

* Broker \= arranges transportation of the freight, for compensation, without transporting it.

* Dispatcher \= an occupational or service label. Whether dispatch activity legally counts as carrier-side agency or as brokerage depends on the actual conduct and relationship involved — not on the title used.

**IMPORTANT:** Calling yourself a dispatcher, or being called one by your clients, does not by itself determine your regulatory status. That's true whether the label helps you or hurts you — titles don't control the legal analysis.

*Source classification: STATUTE (49 U.S.C. § 13102\) / REGULATION (49 CFR § 371.2(a))*

# 6\. Bona Fide Agent — Beginner Version

**FACT-M02-002.** Federal regulation (49 CFR § 371.2(b)) describes a bona fide agent as a person who is part of the normal organization of a motor carrier, performing duties under that carrier's direction pursuant to a preexisting agreement that provides for a continuing relationship — while being precluded from exercising discretion in allocating traffic between that carrier and others.

In plain terms: a bona fide agent works as an extension of one (or more, under conditions covered in Section 10\) motor carrier's own organization, under an ongoing arrangement, and does not get to decide which carrier among several gets a given load.

**IMPORTANT:** The regulation requires a preexisting agreement providing a continuing relationship. It does not say the word "written." FMCSA's separate guidance identifies having a written contractual relationship as an important factor supporting bona fide agent treatment — but the regulatory text itself and the guidance are two different things, and this manual keeps that distinction visible rather than treating the guidance's recommendation as if it were the regulation's requirement.

*Source classification: REGULATION (49 CFR § 371.2(b)), clarified by FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 7\. Dispatch Service Is a Functional Description, Not a Legal Shield

Here's something beginners consistently get wrong: there is no separate federal statute or regulation that defines "dispatch service" as its own legal category with its own rules. The term describes a function in the industry, not a regulatory status.

FMCSA's 2023 final guidance addressed this directly: dispatch-service activities may fall under broker treatment or bona fide-agent treatment, depending on the nature and scope of what the dispatch service actually does and how it's structured — not based on the name "dispatch service" itself.

**IMPORTANT:** What you do, and who you do it for, matters more than what you call yourself. There is no such thing as a federal "dispatcher license" that exists as an alternative to broker authority or bona fide agent status — don't teach or believe otherwise.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 8\. Preexisting Continuing Carrier Relationship

To operate as a bona fide agent rather than a broker, a dispatch service needs a preexisting agreement with the carrier that provides for an ongoing relationship — not a one-time, transaction-by-transaction arrangement with no continuity. FMCSA's guidance identifies a written agreement as an important supporting factor, though the regulation's actual text requires the preexisting-agreement-with-continuing-relationship element rather than specifying a written format.

**Dispatcher application:** A dispatcher entering into a genuine, ongoing working relationship with a carrier — where the dispatcher is functionally part of that carrier's operation over time — looks different, from a regulatory standpoint, than someone picking up one-off jobs for whichever carrier happens to call that day with no ongoing relationship at all.

# 9\. Allocation of Traffic

This is the single most important concept in this module. "Allocating traffic" — the thing a bona fide agent is not allowed to do — means exercising discretion, choice, or decision-making about which represented motor carrier receives a given load.

FMCSA's guidance defines it as any exercise of discretion on an agent's part when assigning a load to a motor carrier. If a dispatch service represents more than one carrier and gets to pick which one hauls a given piece of freight, that decision-making function is exactly what pushes the activity toward brokerage — because deciding who gets the freight is, functionally, what a broker does.

**IMPORTANT:** This is the concept every other section in this module builds around. If you remember one idea from Module 02, make it this one.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023), interpreting REGULATION (49 CFR § 371.2(b))*

# 10\. Representing Multiple Carriers

Representing more than one carrier does NOT automatically turn a dispatch service into a broker. The analysis is fact-specific — it depends on whether representing multiple carriers actually creates the opportunity to allocate traffic between them.
