Payment from the represented motor carrier, under the dispatch relationship, supports carrier-agent treatment. Payment from a broker, a shipper, a factoring company, or otherwise drawn from the transportation transaction's funds may weigh toward brokerage — but only as one factor considered alongside the others in this module, not as an automatic conclusion on its own.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "percentage compensation automatically equals brokerage." It doesn't. It's one fact among several that gets weighed together.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 15. Freight-Money Flow

Handling the money that moves between a shipper and a motor carrier is, according to FMCSA's guidance, a factor that strongly suggests the need for broker authority. But FMCSA has been explicit that handling money is not, by itself, an essential requirement for broker status — it's a strong indicator, not an automatic single-factor test.

Example fact pattern to reason through, not a rule to memorize: a dispatch service receives the freight charge directly from the shipper, keeps its fee, and forwards the remainder to the carrier. That's a strong brokerage-risk pattern — but even here, the full picture (the carrier relationship, the allocation question, the agreement) still matters to the complete analysis.

**IMPORTANT:** Never declare a legal conclusion from one fact in isolation — money flow included. Weigh it alongside the other factors this module covers.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 16. Written Agreements: What They Can and Cannot Do

A properly structured written agreement between a dispatch service and a carrier can document the agency relationship, responsibilities, compensation, authority, limits, and the continuing nature of the relationship. This is valuable — and, per Section 8, it's a factor FMCSA's guidance treats as supporting bona fide agent status.

**IMPORTANT:** A contract's title does not control actual conduct. A document titled "Bona Fide Agent Agreement" does not make allocation-of-traffic activity lawful if what actually happens day to day meets the broker definition. Actual conduct and the complete relationship must be analyzed.

# 17. The 1099 / W-2 Myth

Tax classification does not independently determine broker status. FMCSA's guidance recognizes that a bona fide agent may be structured as either an employee or an independent contractor of the carrier. Being paid on a 1099 doesn't make someone a dispatcher any more than being on a W-2 does — the transportation activity and the carrier relationship are what the analysis actually turns on.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "1099 means dispatcher" or "W-2 is required to be a dispatcher." Neither is accurate.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023)*

# 18. Introduction to Formal Broker Authority

**FACT-M02-006.** For introductory context only — this is not a broker-licensing course. Property brokers generally must satisfy FMCSA registration and financial-responsibility requirements. As of September 2, 2026, 49 CFR § 387.307 requires a broker to maintain $75,000 in financial security, evidenced through Form BMC-84 (surety bond) or Form BMC-85 (trust fund). Broker registration and process-agent requirements are separate registration controls.

**CURRENT 2026 RULE — REVERIFIED SEPTEMBER 2, 2026.** Section 387.307 became effective January 16, 2026. Keep the following clocks separate because they govern different events:

1. **Ineligible BMC-85 provider — 30-day replacement period under current FMCSA guidance.** When FMCSA determines a trust provider is ineligible, an affected broker or freight forwarder relying on that filing has 30 days to obtain a replacement filing from a qualified provider; failure to replace it can result in suspension of operating authority.
2. **Broker response to FMCSA pending-suspension notice — 7 business days from service.** Under 49 CFR § 387.307(e)(5)-(6), after the required provider notification, FMCSA serves written notice that authority will be suspended within 7 business days unless the broker provides qualifying evidence, such as evidence that the notification was erroneous, the security has been restored to $75,000, or pending claims were satisfied without using security assets. If the broker fails to respond within 7 business days of service, FMCSA enters the suspension. This is more precise than describing the rule as a generic "seven-day replenishment clock."
3. **Trust-asset liquidity — 7 calendar days.** Under § 387.307(b), a BMC-85 trust must contain $75,000 in acceptable assets that can be liquidated to cash within 7 calendar days. This is an asset-quality/liquidity requirement, not the broker's response period after an FMCSA notice.
4. **Provider notification — 2 business days.** Under § 387.307(e)(4), a surety company or financial institution must notify FMCSA within 2 business days of the specified payment or determination described in § 387.307(e).

**IMPORTANT:** Do not collapse these into one "seven-day rule." The 30-day provider-replacement period, 7-business-day FMCSA notice-response/suspension process, 7-calendar-day trust-asset liquidity standard, and 2-business-day provider-notification requirement answer different questions.

The purpose of this section is narrow: to show students that broker status is a distinct, separately regulated role with registration and financial-responsibility obligations — not to teach the mechanics of becoming a broker.

*Source classification: REGULATION (49 CFR § 387.307, effective January 16, 2026; as amended through July 21, 2026) / FMCSA GUIDANCE (Broker and Freight Forwarder Financial Responsibility Rule FAQs, revised June 26, 2026). Reverified September 2, 2026.*

# 19. Unauthorized Brokerage: Consequences

**FACT-M02-007.** Federal law, 49 U.S.C. § 14916, prohibits providing interstate brokerage services as a broker unless the person is registered under and compliant with 49 U.S.C. § 13904 and has satisfied the financial-security requirements under § 13906, subject to statutory exceptions.

A person who knowingly authorizes, consents to, or permits a violation is liable to the United States for the applicable civil penalty and separately to an injured party for valid claims without regard to amount. Section 14916 applies liability jointly and severally to the involved corporate entity or partnership and to individual officers, directors, and principals of such entities.

**CURRENT PENALTY — REVERIFIED SEPTEMBER 2, 2026.** The statutory text states a maximum of $10,000 per knowing violation. The current inflation-adjusted FMCSA schedule in 49 CFR Part 386, Appendix B(g)(2), supersedes that unadjusted amount for the federal civil penalty and states a maximum of **$13,676 for each violation** for a person who knowingly operates as a broker in violation of 49 U.S.C. § 13904 registration requirements or § 13906 financial-security requirements.

This amount is date-sensitive. Students should learn the liability framework, not memorize $13,676 as a permanent number. Reverify the current penalty schedule at the commercial release gate and whenever this module is materially updated.

This section does **not** teach that a particular personal asset will automatically be seized. Section 14916 establishes liability; collection/enforcement mechanics are separate matters.

*Source classification: STATUTE (49 U.S.C. § 14916) / REGULATION (49 CFR Part 386, Appendix B(g)(2)). Reverified September 2, 2026.*

# 20. Common Dispatching Legal Myths

### Myth 1: "Dispatchers never need broker authority."

**Why someone may believe it:** The carrier-side dispatcher role is built around agency for a carrier, so it's easy to assume the broker rules simply don't apply to dispatchers at all.

**Correct principle:** Whether broker authority is needed depends on the actual conduct and relationship — not on the dispatcher label. FMCSA's final regulatory guidance expressly discusses dispatch services and circumstances that may indicate broker authority is required.

**Why it matters:** Assuming blanket immunity from broker rules can lead a dispatch service into arranging activity without recognizing the regulatory boundary.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 2: "A dispatcher may only represent one carrier."

**Why someone may believe it:** Some read the bona fide-agent regulation narrowly and conclude a single-carrier limit must be built in.

**Correct principle:** The regulation does not establish a numerical carrier limit. Multiple-carrier representation requires a fact-specific analysis, particularly whether the agent exercises discretion in allocating traffic between the carrier and others.

**Why it matters:** Neither "one carrier only" nor "multiple carriers are automatically safe" is a reliable rule.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 3: "A dispatcher may legally represent up to three carriers."

**Why someone may believe it:** This number circulates informally in the industry as though it were an official safe harbor.

**Correct principle:** The statute, regulation, and FMCSA final guidance do not create a three-carrier numerical safe harbor. The analysis turns on the actual relationship and conduct, including allocation discretion.

**Why it matters:** An invented number can conceal a real allocation problem.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 4: "A dispatch agreement automatically protects you."

**Why someone may believe it:** A written agreement feels like a legal shield.

**Correct principle:** A written agreement is relevant evidence, but the complete relationship and actual conduct must still be analyzed. A label cannot convert contradictory conduct into carrier-side agency.

**Why it matters:** Paperwork is not a substitute for operating within the verified role.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 5: "A 1099 independent contractor is automatically a dispatcher."

**Why someone may believe it:** Tax status and transportation role are often conflated.

**Correct principle:** FMCSA guidance recognizes that a bona fide agent may be an employee or contractor. Tax classification does not independently decide broker status.

**Why it matters:** The analysis must return to the carrier relationship and actual activity.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 6: "Getting paid a percentage automatically makes you a broker."

**Why someone may believe it:** Percentage-based compensation resembles some brokerage compensation structures.

**Correct principle:** Compensation structure is relevant evidence but is not a standalone broker-status test.

**Why it matters:** The complete fact pattern still controls.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 7: "If you never touch the freight, you cannot be a broker."

**Why someone may believe it:** Physical possession gets confused with arranging transportation.

**Correct principle:** The federal broker definition concerns arranging transportation by motor carrier; physical transportation of the property is not what distinguishes a broker from a carrier-side agent.

**Why it matters:** "I never touch the freight" does not resolve the broker question.

*Source classification: STATUTE / REGULATION*

### Myth 8: "Calling yourself a dispatch service prevents broker classification."

**Why someone may believe it:** “Dispatch service” is treated informally as though it were a separate protected federal category.

**Correct principle:** FMCSA's final guidance expressly addresses dispatch services. The nature and scope of the activity and relationship matter, not the business label alone.

**Why it matters:** This module teaches conduct-based issue spotting rather than title-based conclusions.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 9: "A dispatcher can freely move a booked load from one client carrier to another."

**Why someone may believe it:** Reassignment can feel like ordinary customer service when the original carrier cannot perform.

**Correct principle:** Selecting a different independent represented carrier for the same freight can introduce the allocation-of-traffic concern. It is materially different from returning or canceling the opportunity to its source.

**Why it matters:** A well-intentioned replacement decision can change the regulatory analysis.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*
