Payment from the represented motor carrier, under the dispatch relationship, supports carrier-agent treatment. Payment from a broker, a shipper, a factoring company, or otherwise drawn from the transportation transaction's funds may weigh toward brokerage — but only as one factor considered alongside the others in this module, not as an automatic conclusion on its own.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "percentage compensation automatically equals brokerage." It doesn't. It's one fact among several that gets weighed together.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023\)*

# 15\. Freight-Money Flow

Handling the money that moves between a shipper and a motor carrier is, according to FMCSA's guidance, a factor that strongly suggests the need for broker authority. But FMCSA has been explicit that handling money is not, by itself, an essential requirement for broker status — it's a strong indicator, not an automatic single-factor test.

Example fact pattern to reason through, not a rule to memorize: a dispatch service receives the freight charge directly from the shipper, keeps its fee, and forwards the remainder to the carrier. That's a strong brokerage-risk pattern — but even here, the full picture (the carrier relationship, the allocation question, the agreement) still matters to the complete analysis.

**IMPORTANT:** Never declare a legal conclusion from one fact in isolation — money flow included. Weigh it alongside the other factors this module covers.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023\)*

# 16\. Written Agreements: What They Can and Cannot Do

A properly structured written agreement between a dispatch service and a carrier can document the agency relationship, responsibilities, compensation, authority, limits, and the continuing nature of the relationship. This is valuable — and, per Section 8, it's a factor FMCSA's guidance treats as supporting bona fide agent status.

**IMPORTANT:** A contract's title does not control actual conduct. A document titled "Bona Fide Agent Agreement" does not make allocation-of-traffic activity lawful if what actually happens day to day meets the broker definition. Regulators and courts look at conduct, not just paperwork labels.

# 17\. The 1099 / W-2 Myth

Tax classification does not independently determine broker status. FMCSA's guidance recognizes that a bona fide agent may be structured as either an employee or an independent contractor of the carrier. Being paid on a 1099 doesn't make someone a dispatcher any more than being on a W-2 does — the transportation activity and the carrier relationship are what the analysis actually turns on.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "1099 means dispatcher" or "W-2 is required to be a dispatcher." Neither is accurate.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE (88 FR 39368, June 16, 2023\)*

# 18\. Introduction to Formal Broker Authority

**FACT-M02-006.** For introductory context only — this is not a broker-licensing course. Property brokers must generally satisfy FMCSA registration requirements, which as of 2026 include broker operating authority registration, designation of a process agent using Form BOC-3, and financial security of $75,000 using either a surety bond (Form BMC-84) or a qualifying trust fund (Form BMC-85).

CURRENT 2026 RULE — VERIFIED AUGUST 27, 2026: Effective January 16, 2026, FMCSA updated the eligibility requirements for entities that may serve as BMC-85 trust providers. This section covers three distinct timelines — keep them separate, because they answer three different questions:

1. **Trustee ineligibility — 30-day cure window.** If FMCSA determines that a trust provider is ineligible, an affected broker or freight forwarder generally has 30 days to obtain a replacement filing from a qualified provider or face suspension of operating authority.
2. **Financial-security shortfall — 7 business days after notice.** Separately, when available financial security falls below $75,000, the broker or freight forwarder generally has seven **business** days after FMCSA notice to replenish the security before suspension procedures apply.
3. **Trust-asset liquidity — 7 calendar days.** This is a different requirement from either of the above: qualifying BMC-85 trust assets must independently satisfy the rule's liquidity requirements, including the ability to be liquidated to cash within seven **calendar** days. This is an asset-quality standard the trust must meet on an ongoing basis — not a response deadline triggered by an FMCSA notice.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not interchange "business days" and "calendar days" across these three rules — they are not the same timeline measured two ways. The 30-day cure window, the 7-business-day shortfall-replenishment window, and the 7-calendar-day trust-asset-liquidity standard are three separate requirements answering three separate questions.

Separately, current FMCSA compliance guidance identifies a two-business-day notification requirement for trust providers or sureties to notify FMCSA of specified security-reduction events — this is a notification obligation placed on the *provider*, distinct from the broker's own replenishment or cure timelines above.

The purpose of this section is narrow: to show students that broker status is a distinct, separately regulated role with its own registration and financial-security obligations — not to teach the mechanics of becoming a broker.

Source classification: REGULATION (49 CFR § 387.307) — current requirements effective January 16, 2026; verified August 27, 2026\.

# 19\. Unauthorized Brokerage: Consequences

**FACT-M02-007.** Federal law (49 U.S.C. § 14916\) establishes a civil-penalty framework for unauthorized brokerage activity. A person who knowingly authorizes, consents to, or permits unauthorized brokerage is liable to the United States government for a civil penalty, and separately liable to any injured party for valid claims, without a cap tied to that second category.

This liability applies jointly and severally to the corporate entity or partnership involved, and individually to the officers, directors, and principals of that entity.

CURRENT 2026 PENALTY — VERIFIED AUGUST 27, 2026: The statute states a maximum civil penalty of $10,000 per knowing violation, while the current inflation-adjusted FMCSA maximum is **$13,676 per violation under 49 CFR Part 386, Appendix B(g)(2)**. This amount is date-sensitive and can change through future civil-penalty inflation adjustments. Students should learn the liability framework rather than memorize one permanent number; the Academy must recheck the current FMCSA penalty schedule at the final publication/release gate.

Two things this section deliberately does not do: it does not use the phrase "piercing the corporate veil" (that phrase does not appear in the statute's text, even though some commentary uses it informally), and it does not tell students that specific personal assets — a house, a car, a bank account — will necessarily be seized. The statute establishes a liability framework; it does not itself describe asset seizure mechanics, which depend on separate collection proceedings.

*Source classification: STATUTE (49 U.S.C. § 14916) / REGULATION (49 CFR Part 386, Appendix B(g)(2))*

# 20\. Common Dispatching Legal Myths

### Myth 1: "Dispatchers never need broker authority."

**Why someone may believe it:** The carrier-side dispatcher role is built around agency for a carrier, so it's easy to assume the broker rules simply don't apply to dispatchers at all.

**Correct principle:** Whether broker authority is needed depends on the actual conduct and relationship — not on the dispatcher label. A dispatch service that drifts into arranging transportation generally, or allocating traffic between carriers, can trigger broker requirements regardless of its title.

**Why it matters:** Assuming blanket immunity from broker rules is exactly the mindset that leads dispatch services into unauthorized brokerage without realizing it.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 2: "A dispatcher may only represent one carrier."

**Why someone may believe it:** Some read the bona fide agent regulation narrowly and conclude a single-carrier limit must be built in.

**Correct principle:** There is no numerical limit in the regulation or guidance. Representing multiple carriers is fine when it doesn't create allocation-of-traffic discretion — see Section 11\.

**Why it matters:** Believing a hard one-carrier rule exists can cause a dispatcher to either needlessly limit their business, or, in the opposite direction, assume any multi-carrier setup is automatically fine as long as it's more than one — neither assumption is correct.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 3: "A dispatcher may legally represent up to three carriers."

**Why someone may believe it:** This specific number circulates informally in the industry, sometimes presented as an official safe harbor.

**Correct principle:** No such numerical safe harbor exists in the statute, regulation, or FMCSA guidance. The analysis is about allocation of traffic, not carrier count.

**Why it matters:** Relying on an invented number instead of the actual factors (overlap, discretion, relationship) creates false confidence in situations that may still carry real brokerage risk.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 4: "A dispatch agreement automatically protects you."

**Why someone may believe it:** A well-drafted written agreement feels like a legal shield, especially if it's labeled correctly.

**Correct principle:** Contract labels do not control actual conduct. An agreement titled "Bona Fide Agent Agreement" doesn't make allocation-of-traffic activity lawful if that's what's actually happening.

**Why it matters:** Treating paperwork as a substitute for correct conduct is one of the more dangerous myths in this list, because it creates a false sense of security.

*Source classification: REGULATION / FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 5: "A 1099 independent contractor is automatically a dispatcher."

**Why someone may believe it:** Tax status and job function get conflated in casual industry conversation.

**Correct principle:** FMCSA guidance recognizes a bona fide agent may be an employee or a contractor. Tax classification doesn't independently determine broker status.

**Why it matters:** This myth can distract from the factors that actually matter — the carrier relationship and the nature of the activity.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 6: "Getting paid a percentage automatically makes you a broker."

**Why someone may believe it:** Percentage-based fees resemble how some brokers are paid, so the fee structure alone feels disqualifying.

**Correct principle:** No single fee structure automatically determines broker status. Compensation source is one factor among several, not a standalone test.

**Why it matters:** This myth can cause a legitimately structured dispatch service to abandon a normal compensation model out of unnecessary fear.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 7: "If you never touch the freight, you cannot be a broker."

**Why someone may believe it:** "Brokers move money and paperwork, not trucks" gets flipped into "if I never touch anything physical, I'm not a broker."

**Correct principle:** Brokers by definition don't transport freight themselves — that's true of every broker. Not touching the freight is consistent with being a broker, not evidence against it.

**Why it matters:** This myth misunderstands the broker definition entirely — not transporting freight is the broker's normal condition, not a defense.

*Source classification: STATUTE / REGULATION*

### Myth 8: "Calling yourself a dispatch service prevents broker classification."

**Why someone may believe it:** If "dispatch service" isn't defined in federal law, some assume that means it's outside federal law's reach.

**Correct principle:** FMCSA guidance directly addresses this: dispatch-service activities may fall into broker or bona fide-agent treatment depending on the nature and scope of the activity — not the label used.

**Why it matters:** This is the myth this entire module works hardest to correct — activity and relationship control, not title.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*

### Myth 9: "A dispatcher can freely move a booked load from one client carrier to another."

**Why someone may believe it:** If a carrier can't perform, reassigning the load to another represented carrier feels like normal problem-solving.

**Correct principle:** Substituting one independent represented carrier for another after a booking is exactly the kind of decision-making that can constitute allocation of traffic — this materially changes the analysis compared to simply canceling and returning the opportunity. See Scenario 6 in Section 22\.

**Why it matters:** This is a realistic, easy-to-miss scenario where good customer-service instincts can create real brokerage risk.

*Source classification: FMCSA GUIDANCE*
