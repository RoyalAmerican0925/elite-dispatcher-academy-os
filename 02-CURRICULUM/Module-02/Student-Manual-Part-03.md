Do not assume a title — "dispatcher," "dispatch service," "bona fide agent" — makes an activity legal. Pause before proceeding.

### IDENTIFY

Who are you representing in this transaction? One carrier you have an ongoing relationship with, or are you acting more broadly?

### CHECK

* Who owns the freight opportunity — did it come to you through your represented carrier, or independently?

* Who selected the carrier for this load — you, exercising discretion among options, or was there really only one carrier who could take it?

* Who pays you, and from what source — the carrier, or funds moving through the transportation transaction itself?

* Are multiple represented carriers competing for the same load, with you deciding who gets it?

* Are you receiving and handling freight funds between a shipper and a carrier?

* Are you soliciting freight for one named, represented carrier — or offering to arrange transportation generally?

### ESCALATE

If the facts create real uncertainty about whether broker authority is required, stop before proceeding and get qualified regulatory or legal guidance rather than guessing.

**IMPORTANT:** This tool is educational issue-spotting to help you recognize when a situation needs a closer look. It is not individualized legal advice, and using it correctly does not guarantee any particular legal outcome.

# 22\. Scenario Lab

### Scenario 1: One-Carrier Load-Board Sourcing

*All names and companies are fictional.*

Dispatcher Renata has an ongoing agreement with Hallmark Freightways, a single for-hire carrier. She searches a load board daily for freight that fits Hallmark's trucks and books loads exclusively on Hallmark's behalf.

**Classification:** Consistent with carrier-side agency, subject to the complete relationship and conduct.

Renata represents one carrier under an ongoing relationship and isn't allocating traffic between competing carriers. This is the clearest example of low-risk carrier-side dispatching in this module — though even here, the full relationship (agreement, compensation source, conduct) is still what ultimately controls, not just the single-carrier fact alone.

### Scenario 2: General Shipper Solicitation

*All names and companies are fictional.*

A dispatch service sends shippers a message stating: "I can arrange transportation for your freight using my network of carriers."

**Classification:** Raises broker concerns.

This is an offer to arrange transportation generally, not sourcing for one specific represented carrier. It closely tracks the broker definition in Section 5 — "offers to arrange transportation... for compensation" — before any specific load is even booked.

### Scenario 3: Non-Overlapping Equipment

*All names and companies are fictional.*

A dispatch service represents Carrier A (reefer-equipped only) and Carrier B (flatbed-equipped only). A frozen-freight opportunity comes in that only reefer equipment can haul.

**Classification:** Lower allocation-of-traffic risk due to non-overlap.

Because only Carrier A can physically haul frozen freight, there's no real discretion being exercised in "choosing" between the two carriers for this load — the equipment requirement makes the choice for you, which is the kind of non-overlap FMCSA's guidance treats as reducing allocation concerns.

### Scenario 4: Overlapping Carriers

*All names and companies are fictional.*

A dispatch service represents three dry-van carriers, all capable of hauling a given lane. The dispatcher picks one to receive the load.

**Classification:** Raises allocation-of-traffic concerns.

All three carriers could reasonably haul this freight, and the dispatcher is the one deciding which does. This is the textbook allocation-of-traffic fact pattern described in Sections 9 and 11\.

### Scenario 5: Breakdown and Cancellation

*All names and companies are fictional.*

Carrier A, the only carrier represented for this load, experiences a mechanical breakdown and cannot perform. The dispatcher cancels the load and returns the opportunity to the broker who originally offered it.

**Classification:** Lower risk — consistent with carrier-side agency.

The dispatcher isn't redirecting the freight to another represented carrier; the opportunity simply returns to where it came from. No allocation decision was made among competing represented carriers.

### Scenario 6: Breakdown and Reassignment

*All names and companies are fictional.*

Carrier A cannot perform due to a breakdown. Instead of canceling, the dispatcher substitutes Carrier B — a different, independent carrier the dispatch service also represents — to complete the load.

**Classification:** Materially different, higher-risk analysis.

This looks helpful, but it changes the fact pattern significantly: the dispatcher just decided which of two independent represented carriers gets this freight. That decision is functionally an allocation-of-traffic choice, even though it arose from a breakdown rather than routine load selection. See Myth 9 in Section 20\.

### Scenario 7: Contract Title vs. Conduct

*All names and companies are fictional.*

A dispatch service has a signed agreement titled "Bona Fide Agent Agreement" with each carrier it represents. In daily practice, however, the dispatcher regularly decides which of several overlapping carriers receives each incoming load.

**Classification:** Fact-specific / gray leaning toward brokerage risk.

The contract's title doesn't control here — see Section 16\. If the actual day-to-day conduct meets the allocation-of-traffic pattern, the "Bona Fide Agent Agreement" label does not change the underlying analysis.

### Scenario 8: Compensation Source Comparison

*All names and companies are fictional.*

Compare two dispatch services: Service X is paid a monthly fee directly by the one carrier it represents. Service Y receives the freight charge from the shipper, deducts its fee, and forwards the balance to the carrier.

**Classification:** Service X: consistent with carrier-agent treatment. Service Y: strong brokerage-risk indicator.

Service X's payment source supports a carrier-agent relationship (Section 14). Service Y is handling freight funds between the shipper and carrier — a strong indicator under FMCSA guidance, though still only one factor in a complete analysis (Section 15), not an automatic conclusion by itself.
