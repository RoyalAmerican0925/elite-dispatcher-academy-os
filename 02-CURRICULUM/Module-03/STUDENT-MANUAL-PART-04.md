**Why it sounds plausible:** 53-foot dry vans are so standard that it's easy to assume a standard payload comes with them.

**Correct principle:** Actual available payload depends on the specific trailer's tare weight, the tractor's weight, and the applicable gross and axle limits for that specific combination — not a fixed number tied to trailer length.

**Why it matters:** Assuming a fixed payload figure can lead directly to an overweight or axle-noncompliant load — see Sections 17–19.

*Classification: MANUFACTURER-SPECIFIC*

### Myth 2: "80,000 lb means cargo can weigh 80,000 lb."

**Why it sounds plausible:** The number is so commonly cited as "the trucking weight limit" that people assume it refers to freight alone.

**Correct principle:** 80,000 lb (FACT-M03-001) is the combined gross weight of the entire loaded vehicle combination — tractor, trailer, and cargo together, not cargo alone.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the most consequential myths in trucking — confusing gross weight with cargo weight leads directly to overweight loads.

*Classification: LAW*

### Myth 3: "All 53-foot trailers have identical dimensions."

**Why it sounds plausible:** A shared nominal length suggests shared everything else.

**Correct principle:** Usable interior length, width, height, and door opening size vary by manufacturer and by trailer condition/configuration — nominal length is a starting point, not a guarantee.

**Why it matters:** Assuming identical dimensions can cause a freight-to-trailer mismatch that wasn't actually necessary — see Section 16.

*Classification: MANUFACTURER-SPECIFIC*

### Myth 4: "Reefers have the same usable space as dry vans."

**Why it sounds plausible:** Both are enclosed box trailers of similar nominal size.

**Correct principle:** Reefer insulation reduces usable interior space compared to a similarly sized dry van, and reefer units are typically heavier — reducing available cargo weight too.

**Why it matters:** Assuming equal capacity between reefer and dry van equipment can cause both a space and a weight-margin miscalculation.

*Classification: MANUFACTURER-SPECIFIC*

### Myth 5: "Flatbeds can haul anything that fits on the deck."

**Why it sounds plausible:** If it physically sits on the deck without hanging off, it feels like it should be fine to move.

**Correct principle:** Fitting on the deck says nothing about legal loaded height, required securement, weight distribution, or route feasibility — see Section 9.

**Why it matters:** This myth skips directly past the legal and safety analysis that actually determines whether a flatbed load can move.

*Classification: INDUSTRY PRACTICE*

### Myth 6: "Federal law sets one nationwide vehicle-height maximum."

**Why it sounds plausible:** Width has a clear federal number, so it's natural to assume height works the same way.

**Correct principle:** There is no comparable federal height maximum (FACT-M03-004). Height limits are primarily state-controlled, and common figures like 13'6" or 14' are state examples, not a federal rule.

**Why it matters:** Assuming a single nationwide height limit can cause a dispatcher to miss a state-specific clearance problem on a tall load.

*Classification: STATE-SPECIFIC*

### Myth 7: "A dispatcher only needs freight weight, not dimensions."

**Why it sounds plausible:** Weight feels like the "big number" that determines whether a load can move.

**Correct principle:** Both weight and dimensions independently determine fit — a load can be well under weight limits and still not physically fit the assigned equipment, or vice versa.

**Why it matters:** This is the module's core lesson in miniature: weight alone is never the whole picture.

*Classification: INDUSTRY PRACTICE*

### Myth 8: "Power-only means the carrier provides the trailer."

**Why it sounds plausible:** "Power-only" sounds like it could describe either party supplying either piece of equipment.

**Correct principle:** Power-only means the carrier supplies only the tractor; the trailer comes from another party.

**Why it matters:** Getting this backwards can cause a dispatcher to assume trailer availability that doesn't actually exist.

*Classification: INDUSTRY PRACTICE*

### Myth 9: "Hotshot means any load moved with a pickup."

**Why it sounds plausible:** The term gets used loosely for any smaller truck-and-trailer setup.

**Correct principle:** "Hotshot" specifically refers to time-sensitive freight operations, commonly using heavy-duty pickups and gooseneck trailers — and which specific federal requirements apply depends on configuration, weight ratings, and other fact-specific conditions, not the term itself.

**Why it matters:** Treating "hotshot" as a self-defining regulatory category can cause a dispatcher to miss which specific federal rules actually apply.

*Classification: INDUSTRY PRACTICE*

### Myth 10: "Dry vans are always dock-loaded."

**Why it sounds plausible:** Dock-loading is by far the most common method, so it's easy to assume it's the only one.

**Correct principle:** Most dry vans are dock/rear-loaded, but a specific load requiring side-loading may simply be incompatible with a particular conventional enclosed dry van.

**Why it matters:** Assuming universal dock-loading capability can cause a dispatcher to book a load the assigned equipment can't actually accommodate.

*Classification: INDUSTRY PRACTICE*

# 29\. Scenario Lab

### S-03-01: Standard Pallets, Unclear Details

A shipper offers 22 standard 48x40 pallets for a dry van. No further details are provided.

**Classification:** NEEDS MORE INFORMATION / POTENTIAL FIT.

22 standard pallets is common dry-van freight, but critical facts are missing: freight height, overhang or unusual orientation, the trailer's actual usable interior dimensions, and weight distribution. A dispatcher should request these before booking rather than assuming a standard load fits a standard trailer.

### S-03-02: Bottled Water Weight Check

A load of 44,500 lb bottled water is offered. The carrier's actual operating equipment weighs 37,000 lb.

**Classification:** BAD FIT / OVERWEIGHT RISK under the supplied facts.

44,500 + 37,000 = 81,500 lb total — over the 80,000 lb federal Interstate gross baseline based on the numbers given. This load cannot move as described without a change in equipment, freight quantity, or applicable permit/exception.

### S-03-03: Produce to a Dry Van

Temperature-controlled produce is offered to a conventional dry van.

**Classification:** BAD FIT.

A dry van has no active temperature-control unit. Temperature-sensitive produce needs a reefer. This is an equipment-type mismatch, not a dimensions-or-weight problem.

### S-03-04: Side-Loaded Lumber to Conventional Dry Van

A shipper requires side-loading for a lumber shipment. The assigned equipment is a conventional enclosed dry van with only rear doors.

**Classification:** BAD FIT for the specified equipment/loading method.

A conventional dry van without side access can't accommodate a side-loading requirement.

### S-03-05: Oversize Generator on Standard Flatbed

A 12-foot-tall industrial generator is offered against a standard flatbed with an approximately 5-foot deck height. The approximate loaded height would be 17 feet before considering other configuration details.

**Classification:** SPECIALIZED / ESCALATE.

A roughly 17-foot loaded height would be far outside ordinary legal-height operation in many jurisdictions. The dispatcher must stop and obtain specialized equipment analysis, state-specific permit review, and route/clearance analysis.

### S-03-06: Steel Coils Without Required Chains

A customer's contract requires chain-and-binder securement for a steel coil load. The assigned carrier does not currently have chains or binders on hand.

**Classification:** BAD FIT — currently.

This is a real gap, but it's a customer contractual requirement, not necessarily a federal securement sufficiency question — the two are different standards. The load can potentially become a fit once the carrier obtains the required equipment.

### S-03-07: Rigid Freight vs. Door Opening

A rigid, non-flexible piece of freight measures 108 inches wide. The assigned trailer's door opening measures 104 inches.

**Classification:** BAD FIT.
