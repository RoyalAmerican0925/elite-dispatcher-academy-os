Intermodal freight moves in standardized shipping containers that can transfer between ship, rail, and truck without unloading the freight itself. An intermodal chassis is the wheeled trailer frame that a container sits on for the road portion of the trip. Compatibility between a specific container and a specific chassis, along with any required interchange paperwork, are dispatcher-level concerns for this equipment type.

# 15\. Power-Only

Power-only generally means the carrier supplies only the tractor (power unit) to move a trailer that's supplied by another party — the carrier does not own or supply the trailer itself.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "power-only means the carrier supplies the trailer." It's the opposite — the trailer comes from someone else. Dispatcher questions for a power-only load include: What exact trailer type is being provided? What condition is it in? Is it mechanically compatible with the assigned tractor? What connection, interchange, or contract requirements apply? What's the pickup/drop procedure, and does trailer inspection documentation apply?

# 16\. Understanding Freight Dimensions

Every load has a length, width, and height — and every trailer has a usable interior space that may be smaller than its nominal advertised size. Before booking, a dispatcher needs actual freight dimensions and actual usable trailer dimensions, not assumptions based on trailer nickname alone.

**IMPORTANT:** A trailer marketed or referred to as "53-foot" does not guarantee 53 feet of usable interior length, and its door opening may be smaller than its interior height. Nominal size is a starting point for investigation, not a fact you can book against directly.

# 17\. Understanding Weight

Weight isn't one number — it's several related numbers that mean different things, and mixing them up is one of the most consequential mistakes a new dispatcher can make.

## 18\. GVW, GVWR, GCWR, Tare & Payload Concepts

* Gross Vehicle Weight (GVW) — the actual total weight of a vehicle or combination and any load on it, at a given moment.

* Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR) — the maximum operating weight a vehicle is rated for by its manufacturer/regulatory rating, based on the vehicle's design and component ratings — not simply a casual "safe weight" figure.

* Gross Combination Weight Rating (GCWR) — the maximum rated weight of a tractor and trailer combination together, including cargo.

* Tare weight — the weight of the empty vehicle or trailer by itself, without cargo.

* Payload — the weight of cargo a vehicle can carry, calculated relative to its actual tare weight and the applicable weight rating or legal limit.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not define GVWR merely as "the manufacturer's maximum safe weight" in casual terms — it's a specific rating concept tied to the vehicle's design and applicable regulatory framework, and casual "safe weight" language understates that. Similarly, do not assume tare weight automatically includes a specific fuel condition (full tank, half tank, etc.) unless the specific weight figure or scenario states which condition it reflects — a vehicle's actual weight changes with fuel load, and assuming one standard condition without confirmation can produce an inaccurate weight-margin calculation.

# 19\. Axle Weight & Bridge Formula Awareness

**FACT-M03-001.** The general federal gross vehicle weight baseline on the Interstate System is 80,000 pounds.

**IMPORTANT:** This is the combined actual weight of the loaded vehicle combination — tractor, trailer, and cargo together. It is NOT "the truck can carry 80,000 pounds of freight," and being under 80,000 lb total does not, by itself, prove a load is legal. Axle weights, Bridge Formula requirements, equipment ratings, state/route restrictions, permits, and load distribution all still matter.

**FACT-M03-002.** Within that 80,000 lb baseline, the federal Interstate limits are 20,000 lb on a single axle and 34,000 lb on a tandem axle group, subject to Federal Bridge Formula requirements, axle spacing rules, and applicable exceptions.

Here's the beginner lesson that trips people up: a truck can weigh 79,000 pounds total — under the 80,000 lb baseline — and still have an illegally overweight axle group, if the weight isn't distributed correctly across the axles.

### The Federal Bridge Formula, conceptually

Legal weight isn't just about the total — it's also affected by how many axles carry the load and how far apart they're spaced. More axles, spread farther apart, can legally support more weight; fewer axles, or axles bunched closely together, support less. This is why two trucks can have the identical gross weight and identical axle counts, yet one is legal and the other isn't, depending on axle spacing.

**IMPORTANT:** This module does not turn into an advanced Bridge Formula calculation course. The essential beginner lesson is: gross weight alone is not enough. Do not teach students to physically move tandems or adjust fifth wheels as a mechanical procedure — teach them to recognize a weight-distribution problem and flag it as CORRECTION REQUIRED BEFORE MOVEMENT, not something the dispatcher personally resolves at the yard.

*Classification: FEDERAL LAW (23 CFR § 658.17) — Interstate System*

# 20\. Federal Width / Length Framework

**FACT-M03-003.** On the National Network of highways, federal rules generally require states to permit commercial vehicles up to 102 inches wide, and states generally may not impose a semitrailer-length limit of less than 48 feet for a conventional tractor-semitrailer combination.

States may permit longer semitrailers, and most do — 53-foot semitrailers are common industry equipment, but 53 feet is NOT a universal federal maximum; it's simply the size most states have chosen to allow above the 48-foot federal floor.

**IMPORTANT:** Always keep these four categories distinct in your own thinking: the FEDERAL REQUIREMENT (the 48-foot floor and 102-inch width), the STATE-PERMITTED SIZE (often larger, and varies by state), the COMMON EQUIPMENT SIZE (53 feet, as an industry norm), and the MANUFACTURER DIMENSION (the actual trailer in front of you). These are four different things, and treating any one of them as automatically equal to another is a common beginner error.

*Classification: FEDERAL LAW (23 CFR Part 658\) — National Network; \[STATE-SPECIFIC\] for permitted sizes above the federal floor*

# 21\. Height, Oversize & Overweight

**FACT-M03-004.** There is no single federal maximum commercial-vehicle height limit comparable to the federal National Network width rule. Height limits are primarily state-controlled.

**\[STATE-SPECIFIC\]** Figures like 13'6" or 14' may appear as common state examples — but do not teach either one as a nationwide federal rule. Tall freight requires checking the actual loaded height, each applicable state's specific height rule along the route, route clearances (bridges, overpasses, tunnels), permit requirements, and the specific equipment configuration being used.

Oversize and overweight analysis — freight or equipment that exceeds standard legal dimensions or weight — depends on the specific states and routes involved, and typically requires permits. This module teaches recognition only: a dispatcher needs to recognize when a load may require oversize/overweight handling and escalate it, not personally engineer the permit and route.

# 22\. Loading & Unloading Methods

Different equipment types load and unload differently — dock/rear-loaded (most dry vans and reefers), side-loaded (some flatbed, Conestoga, or curtain-side equipment), top/overhead-loaded (flatbed and open-deck equipment, and some Conestoga configurations), and driven-on (RGN equipment for wheeled cargo). A dispatcher needs to confirm that the required loading method actually matches what the assigned equipment and pickup facility can support — see Scenario S-03-04 and S-03-09.

# 23\. Equipment Accessories

These accessories affect whether specific equipment can handle specific freight — a dispatcher needs to recognize what they are and when they matter, without performing the physical securement work personally.

| Accessory | What It Is / Why a Load Might Need It / What to Verify |
| :---- | :---- |
| Liftgate | A powered platform for loading/unloading without a dock. Verify: does the pickup or delivery location lack dock access? |
| Pallet Jack | A manual tool for moving palletized freight short distances. Verify: is the freight palletized, and is a jack available on-site? |
| Load Bars / Load Locks | Adjustable bars that brace freight inside an enclosed trailer to prevent shifting. Verify: does the freight require in-transit bracing? |
| Straps | Fabric tie-downs used to secure freight, especially on open-deck equipment. Verify: does the commodity and equipment require strap securement? |
| Chains | Heavy-duty securement for heavier or metal freight (e.g., steel coils, machinery). Verify: does the customer or commodity require chain securement specifically? See Scenario S-03-06. |
| Binders | Tensioning devices used to tighten chains. Verify: paired with chain requirements above. |
| Tarps | Waterproof covers for open-deck freight. Verify: does the commodity require weather protection? |
| Edge Protectors | Corner/edge guards that prevent straps or chains from cutting into freight. Verify: is the freight vulnerable to strap/chain damage? |
| Dunnage | Loose bracing/padding material placed among freight to prevent shifting or damage. Verify: does the freight type need in-load spacing or cushioning? |
| Ramps | Inclined surfaces for driving or rolling freight on/off equipment. Verify: is the freight wheeled, or does it need a ramp for loading? |
| E-Track | A metal track system built into trailer walls/floors for flexible strap anchoring. Verify: does the trailer have E-Track, and does the load plan rely on it? |
| Air-Ride | A suspension system that cushions freight from road shock. Verify: does the commodity (e.g., fragile or sensitive freight) require air-ride equipment specifically? |
| Swing Doors | Traditional hinged rear doors on a trailer. Verify: does the loading method and dock configuration work with swing doors? |
| Roll-Up Doors | Overhead-style rear doors that roll up rather than swing open. Verify: same as above — confirm dock/loading compatibility. |
| Coil Racks | Trailer-floor cradles designed to secure steel coils. Verify: is this coil freight, and does the trailer have coil racks installed? |
| Reefer Chute | An airflow channel (often a floor or ceiling design) that helps circulate cold air through a reefer trailer. Verify: relevant for temperature-sensitive freight requiring even airflow. |
| Side Kits | Removable side panels that convert a flatbed toward a more enclosed configuration. Verify: does the freight or weather condition call for added side protection? |

**IMPORTANT:** This module teaches recognition and dispatcher-level verification questions only. It does not train physical cargo-securement technique — that is a hands-on skill outside the scope of dispatcher instruction.

# 24\. Temperature-Controlled Freight

Building on Section 7, remember the three distinct temperature concepts — shipper-specified operating temperature, reefer set point, and product/pulp temperature — before booking any temperature-sensitive load. Confirm which one you actually have information about, since assuming they match without confirmation is a common source of claims and rejected freight.
