**ELITE DISPATCHER ACADEMY**

**Student Manual — Module 03**

Trucks, Trailers, Freight & Equipment

*Assignment C-003*

# 1\. Module Introduction

Modules 01 and 02 gave you the industry map and the legal boundaries of your role. Now it's time to get physical: trucks, trailers, and freight. This module teaches you how to look at a load and a piece of equipment and answer one practical question — can this equipment actually, physically, and legally handle this freight?

This isn't a mechanic's course, a cargo-securement certification, or a hazmat class. It's a dispatcher's course. You're learning to recognize fit and to recognize your own limits — knowing when you have enough information to book confidently, and when you need to stop and get more.

**IMPORTANT:** The central rule of this entire module: DO NOT BOOK FROM WEIGHT ALONE. You'll see this idea again and again, in different forms, because it's the single most common beginner mistake this module exists to prevent.

# 2\. Why Equipment Knowledge Matters to Dispatchers

Every load you're offered comes with two sets of facts: what the freight needs, and what your equipment can actually do. A dispatcher who doesn't know the difference between a day cab and a sleeper, or between nominal and usable trailer dimensions, can commit a carrier to a load that can't legally or physically be completed — creating a failed pickup, a damaged customer relationship, or a real safety or legal problem.

**Dispatcher application:** Your job isn't to be an engineer. It's to ask the right questions, recognize red flags, and know when "I need more information" is the correct and professional answer — not a sign of weakness.

# 3\. Tractor vs. Trailer

A tractor (also called a power unit) is the vehicle with the engine — it provides the power and pulls the load. A trailer has no engine; it carries the freight and is pulled by the tractor. Tractors and trailers are typically owned, matched, and swapped independently of each other, which is part of why understanding each separately matters.

# 4\. Day Cab vs. Sleeper

A day cab has no sleeping area behind the driver's seat — just the cab itself. A sleeper tractor includes a berth where a driver can rest without leaving the truck.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "day cab = cannot perform a multi-day load." Federal Hours of Service regulates driving, on-duty, and off-duty time — not whether the cab physically contains a bed. A driver operating a day cab can still take qualifying off-duty time away from the truck where conditions allow it; sleeper-berth provisions are simply a separate, additional method of qualifying for required rest. Full HOS mechanics are covered in Module 11 — for now, just don't assume cab type alone determines what a load can accomplish.

# 5\. Straight Trucks, Box Trucks, Cargo Vans & Hotshot

A straight truck has the engine, cab, and cargo area built on a single frame — there's no separate trailer to connect. A box truck is a common straight-truck configuration with an enclosed box body. A cargo van is a smaller enclosed vehicle, often used for smaller or expedited freight.

"Hotshot" is an industry term, not a federal regulatory category. It generally refers to time-sensitive freight moved using smaller commercial combinations — often heavy-duty pickups pulling gooseneck trailers.

**[FACT-SPECIFIC]** Do not teach that all hotshot operations over 10,000 lb are automatically subject to every FMCSA rule in the same way as a standard tractor-trailer. Different federal requirements apply at different weight thresholds and under different conditions depending on vehicle configuration, weight ratings, interstate operation, commodity, and operating status. Whether a specific requirement applies to a specific hotshot setup needs to be verified for that setup — it's not a single blanket answer.

# 6\. Dry Vans

### Dry Van

**What it is:** An enclosed, unrefrigerated general-freight trailer — the most common trailer type in the industry.

**What it commonly hauls:** Palletized and boxed general freight that doesn't require temperature control.

**How it's generally loaded/unloaded:** Commonly loaded and unloaded at a dock, through rear swing doors or a roll-up door.

**What matters to a dispatcher:** Nominal length (like "53-foot") does not guarantee usable interior length — walls, liner, and structure reduce it. The door opening can also be smaller than the trailer's interior height, which matters for tall freight even if it would otherwise fit inside.

**Questions the dispatcher must ask:** What is the usable interior length, width, and height — not just the nominal size? What is the door opening size? Does the loading method match this equipment?

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "dry vans must always be rear-loaded." That's the common case, but a load requiring side-loading may simply be incompatible with a particular conventional enclosed dry van — loading method still depends on the specific equipment and facility involved. See Scenario S-03-04.

# 7\. Refrigerated Trailers (Reefer)

### Reefer

**What it is:** An enclosed trailer with an active temperature-control unit.

**What it commonly hauls:** Temperature-sensitive freight: perishable food, pharmaceuticals, and similar goods.

**How it's generally loaded/unloaded:** Similar to a dry van (dock/rear-loaded in most cases), but pre-cooling and temperature verification are part of the process.

**What matters to a dispatcher:** Insulation reduces usable interior space compared to a similarly sized dry van. Reefer equipment is often heavier than comparable dry-van equipment, which reduces available cargo weight margin — the exact payload must be verified from the actual operating weight of that specific unit, not assumed.

**Questions the dispatcher must ask:** What is the required operating temperature, and is it different from the product's actual starting temperature? Is the unit's actual tare weight known? Is pre-cooling required before loading?

Three temperature concepts are easy to confuse and should not be treated as automatically identical:

* Shipper-specified operating temperature — what the shipper requires the reefer unit to be set to and/or maintain.
* Reefer set point — what the unit is actually set to run at.
* Product/pulp temperature — the actual internal temperature of the freight itself, which may differ from the surrounding air temperature.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not state a universal reefer payload figure. Reefer units vary significantly in weight, and exact available cargo weight must be verified for the specific unit assigned to the load.

# 8\. Food / Sanitary Transportation

FACT-M03-006. Some food shipments are subject to FDA sanitary-transportation requirements, but whether and how those requirements apply depends on the food type, whether temperature control is required for safety, shipper-allocated responsibilities, and applicable exemptions.

**[FACT-SPECIFIC]** Do not teach "every food load legally requires pre-cooling" or "the reefer must always match the broker's stated set point because federal law says so." Neither is a safe universal statement. Teach students to obtain and follow the specific controlling shipment requirements for that load, and to escalate uncertainty rather than assume a general rule applies. Detailed food-safety compliance is outside this module's scope.

# 9\. Flatbeds & Open-Deck Equipment

### Flatbed

**What it is:** An open, flat trailer bed with no walls or roof.

**What it commonly hauls:** Freight that can tolerate exposure to weather, or that's too large/oddly shaped for an enclosed trailer — building materials, steel, machinery, and similar goods.

**How it's generally loaded/unloaded:** Typically top-loaded from above, using a forklift, crane, or overhead loading equipment, since there are no side or rear walls to load through.

**What matters to a dispatcher:** Deck height plus freight height determines approximate total loaded height, but legal operation still depends on the actual configuration, route, applicable state rules, and any required permits.

**Questions the dispatcher must ask:** What securement does this freight require? Is the deck height and freight height combination within legal and route-safe limits? Does the freight need weather protection?

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "if it fits on the deck, it can move." Fitting physically on the deck says nothing about legal height, securement requirements, or route feasibility.

# 10\. Step Decks, Double Drops, Lowboys & RGN

These are all open-deck variations designed to carry taller freight than a standard flatbed can, by lowering the deck closer to the road:

* Step deck (drop deck) — has a lowered rear deck section behind a raised front section, allowing taller freight in the lower section.
* Double drop — has a lowered center section between two raised ends, for even taller freight.
