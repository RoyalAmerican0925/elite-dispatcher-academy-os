**IMPORTANT:** Always keep these four categories distinct in your own thinking: the FEDERAL REQUIREMENT (the 48-foot floor and 102-inch width), the STATE-PERMITTED SIZE (often larger, and varies by state), the COMMON EQUIPMENT SIZE (53 feet, as an industry norm), and the MANUFACTURER DIMENSION (the actual trailer in front of you). These are four different things, and treating any one of them as automatically equal to another is a common beginner error.

*Classification: FEDERAL LAW (23 CFR Part 658) — National Network; [STATE-SPECIFIC] for permitted sizes above the federal floor*

# 21\. Height, Oversize & Overweight

**FACT-M03-004.** There is no single federal maximum commercial-vehicle height limit comparable to the federal National Network width rule. Height limits are primarily state-controlled.

**[STATE-SPECIFIC]** Figures like 13'6" or 14' may appear as common state examples — but do not teach either one as a nationwide federal rule. Tall freight requires checking the actual loaded height, each applicable state's specific height rule along the route, route clearances, permit requirements, and the specific equipment configuration being used.

Oversize and overweight analysis depends on the specific states and routes involved, and typically requires permits. This module teaches recognition only: a dispatcher needs to recognize when a load may require oversize/overweight handling and escalate it, not personally engineer the permit and route.

# 22\. Loading & Unloading Methods

Different equipment types load and unload differently — dock/rear-loaded, side-loaded, top/overhead-loaded, and driven-on. A dispatcher needs to confirm that the required loading method actually matches what the assigned equipment and pickup facility can support.

# 23\. Equipment Accessories

These accessories affect whether specific equipment can handle specific freight — a dispatcher needs to recognize what they are and when they matter, without performing the physical securement work personally.

| Liftgate | A powered platform for loading/unloading without a dock. Verify: does the pickup or delivery location lack dock access? |

# 24\. Temperature-Controlled Freight

Building on Section 7, remember the three distinct temperature concepts — shipper-specified operating temperature, reefer set point, and product/pulp temperature — before booking any temperature-sensitive load. Confirm which one you actually have information about, since assuming they match without confirmation is a common source of claims and rejected freight.

# 25\. Hazmat Awareness

**FACT-M03-005.** Hazardous materials transportation involves several distinct, specialized federal requirements that apply only in specific circumstances — not as one blanket rule triggered by "any hazmat."

* PHMSA registration applies only to specified categories and quantities of hazardous materials shipments — not to every hazmat movement.
* An H endorsement on a driver's CDL applies in specified regulated circumstances, not universally to every hazmat load.
* FMCSA Hazmat Safety Permits apply only to certain high-hazard materials and quantities.
* Placarding requirements depend on the specific material and quantity involved.

**[FACT-SPECIFIC]** Do not teach "any hazmat = registration + H endorsement + HMSP." The correct dispatcher response to a hazmat load is: STOP, VERIFY, ESCALATE — using the shipment's actual supplied transportation information rather than guessing. Detailed hazmat instruction is outside this module.

# 26\. Load-to-Equipment Fit Checklist

Before booking any load, work through this checklist. It exists because no single fact — especially not weight alone — tells you whether a load fits.

1. Equipment type
2. Freight dimensions
3. Trailer usable dimensions
4. Door / opening clearance
5. Freight weight
6. Actual operating / tare weight of the specific unit
7. Axle / weight-distribution concerns
8. Loading method
9. Unloading method
10. Accessory requirements
11. Commodity restrictions
12. Temperature requirements
13. Hazmat requirements
14. Oversize / overweight / permit possibility
15. Facility restrictions
16. Carrier / driver qualifications
17. Missing information — what don't you know yet?
18. STOP / VERIFY / ESCALATE condition

**IMPORTANT:** Core lesson: DO NOT BOOK FROM WEIGHT ALONE. Weight is one line on an eighteen-line checklist, not the whole checklist.

# 27\. The Weight-Margin Relationship

A useful beginner relationship for thinking through available cargo weight:

**Available gross-weight margin** = applicable gross limit − actual operating weight before cargo.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not call this figure "legal payload" without qualification. This relationship gives you a starting margin, not a guarantee of what can actually, legally be loaded. Actual legal loadability can still be reduced by axle limits, Bridge Formula requirements, equipment ratings, weight distribution, state/route restrictions, and permits or exceptions.
