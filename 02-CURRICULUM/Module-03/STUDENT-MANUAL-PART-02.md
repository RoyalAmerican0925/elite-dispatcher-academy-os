* Lowboy — a heavy-duty low-deck trailer typically used for very tall or very heavy equipment.

* RGN (Removable Gooseneck) — a lowboy variant where the front detaches, allowing equipment to be driven directly onto the deck rather than craned or ramped on.

**IMPORTANT:** The same core principle from Section 9 applies: deck height plus freight height gives an approximate loaded height, but actual legal operation depends on the specific configuration, route, state rules, and permits — not deck height alone.

# 11\. Conestoga & Curtain-Side

A Conestoga is a trailer with a retractable, tarp-covered frame system that can open for loading while still offering weather protection similar to an enclosed trailer. Curtain-side trailers use sliding fabric "curtain" walls instead of solid sides, allowing side loading access.

**[CONFIGURATION-SPECIFIC]** These are configuration-specific. Conestoga systems differ — some retract far enough to allow overhead (crane) loading; others have more limited access. Curtain-side equipment also varies — some have fixed roofs that block overhead loading; others may permit different access. Do not teach "Conestogas cannot be crane loaded" or "curtain-sides cannot be top-loaded" as universal statements. Verify the specific trailer's configuration before assuming what it can or can't do. See Scenario S-03-09.

# 12\. Tankers & Bulk Equipment

Tankers carry liquids or gases in a cylindrical tank body. Hoppers and pneumatic/dry-bulk trailers carry dry bulk materials, typically loaded from the top and unloaded from the bottom or via pneumatic offloading systems.

**[FACT-SPECIFIC]** Tank and bulk equipment often carries commodity-specific cleaning, compatibility, and, for some commodities, hazmat requirements. Treat commodity compatibility as something to verify for the specific product and trailer involved, not something to assume.

# 13\. Dump, Auto Transport, Livestock & Other Specialized Equipment

A dump trailer unloads by hydraulically raising the bed to let freight slide out. An auto transport trailer is a specialized open or enclosed trailer built to carry multiple vehicles.

A livestock trailer is built for the safe transport of live animals and involves considerations well beyond general dry freight.

**[FACT-SPECIFIC]** Livestock transportation can involve specialized animal-welfare requirements, timing requirements, facility requirements, biosecurity/washout requirements, and agricultural transportation rules. This module does not combine these into one universal rule or teach detailed livestock compliance. Treat livestock loads as SPECIALIZED — VERIFY APPLICABLE REQUIREMENTS.

# 14\. Intermodal Equipment

Intermodal freight moves in standardized shipping containers that can transfer between ship, rail, and truck without unloading the freight itself. An intermodal chassis is the wheeled trailer frame that a container sits on for the road portion of the trip. Compatibility between a specific container and a specific chassis, along with any required interchange paperwork, are dispatcher-level concerns for this equipment type.

# 15\. Power-Only

Power-only generally means the carrier supplies only the tractor to move a trailer that's supplied by another party — the carrier does not own or supply the trailer itself.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not teach "power-only means the carrier supplies the trailer." It's the opposite — the trailer comes from someone else. Dispatcher questions include: What exact trailer type is being provided? What condition is it in? Is it mechanically compatible with the assigned tractor? What connection, interchange, or contract requirements apply? What's the pickup/drop procedure, and does trailer inspection documentation apply?

# 16\. Understanding Freight Dimensions

Every load has a length, width, and height — and every trailer has a usable interior space that may be smaller than its nominal advertised size. Before booking, a dispatcher needs actual freight dimensions and actual usable trailer dimensions, not assumptions based on trailer nickname alone.

**IMPORTANT:** A trailer marketed or referred to as "53-foot" does not guarantee 53 feet of usable interior length, and its door opening may be smaller than its interior height. Nominal size is a starting point for investigation, not a fact you can book against directly.

# 17\. Understanding Weight

Weight isn't one number — it's several related numbers that mean different things, and mixing them up is one of the most consequential mistakes a new dispatcher can make.

## 18\. GVW, GVWR, GCWR, Tare & Payload Concepts

* Gross Vehicle Weight (GVW) — the actual total weight of a vehicle or combination and any load on it, at a given moment.
* Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR) — the maximum operating weight a vehicle is rated for by its manufacturer/regulatory rating.
* Gross Combination Weight Rating (GCWR) — the maximum rated weight of a tractor and trailer combination together, including cargo.
* Tare weight — the weight of the empty vehicle or trailer by itself, without cargo.
* Payload — the weight of cargo a vehicle can carry, calculated relative to its actual tare weight and the applicable weight rating or legal limit.

**IMPORTANT:** Do not define GVWR merely as "the manufacturer's maximum safe weight" in casual terms — it's a specific rating concept tied to the vehicle's design and applicable regulatory framework. Similarly, do not assume tare weight automatically includes a specific fuel condition unless the specific weight figure or scenario states which condition it reflects.

# 19\. Axle Weight & Bridge Formula Awareness

**FACT-M03-001.** The general federal gross vehicle weight baseline on the Interstate System is 80,000 pounds.

**IMPORTANT:** This is the combined actual weight of the loaded vehicle combination — tractor, trailer, and cargo together. It is NOT "the truck can carry 80,000 pounds of freight," and being under 80,000 lb total does not, by itself, prove a load is legal. Axle weights, Bridge Formula requirements, equipment ratings, state/route restrictions, permits, and load distribution all still matter.

**FACT-M03-002.** Within that 80,000 lb baseline, the federal Interstate limits are 20,000 lb on a single axle and 34,000 lb on a tandem axle group, subject to Federal Bridge Formula requirements, axle spacing rules, and applicable exceptions.

Here's the beginner lesson that trips people up: a truck can weigh 79,000 pounds total — under the 80,000 lb baseline — and still have an illegally overweight axle group, if the weight isn't distributed correctly across the axles.

### The Federal Bridge Formula, conceptually

Legal weight isn't just about the total — it's also affected by how many axles carry the load and how far apart they're spaced. More axles, spread farther apart, can legally support more weight; fewer axles, or axles bunched closely together, support less.

**IMPORTANT:** This module does not turn into an advanced Bridge Formula calculation course. The essential beginner lesson is: gross weight alone is not enough. Do not teach students to physically move tandems or adjust fifth wheels as a mechanical procedure — teach them to recognize a weight-distribution problem and flag it as CORRECTION REQUIRED BEFORE MOVEMENT.

*Classification: FEDERAL LAW (23 CFR § 658.17) — Interstate System*

# 20\. Federal Width / Length Framework

**FACT-M03-003.** On the National Network of highways, federal rules generally require states to permit commercial vehicles up to 102 inches wide, and states generally may not impose a semitrailer-length limit of less than 48 feet for a conventional tractor-semitrailer combination.

States may permit longer semitrailers, and most do — 53-foot semitrailers are common industry equipment, but 53 feet is NOT a universal federal maximum; it's simply the size most states have chosen to allow above the 48-foot federal floor.
