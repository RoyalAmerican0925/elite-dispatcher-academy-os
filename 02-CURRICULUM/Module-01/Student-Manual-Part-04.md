# MODULE 01 — INDUSTRY FOUNDATIONS & THE DISPATCHER'S ROLE
## Student Manual — Part 04: Reading the Dispatch Desk

A dispatcher does not work with a vague idea called “a load.” A dispatcher works with a changing set of facts about a shipment, a carrier, a driver, equipment, places, times, people, documents, and decisions.

The beginner mistake is to see one line — for example, “Dallas to Tulsa, $1,450” — and believe the load has been understood. It has not. That line is only a fragment of a freight file.

This section teaches you how to look at freight the way an operating desk must look at it: as a set of connected information that must be identified, traced, and verified before it can support a decision.

---

## 1. The four flows inside every load

A useful way to understand transportation is to separate four flows that occur at the same time.

### Flow 1 — Physical freight
This is the movement of the cargo itself.

A simplified path might be:

**shipper/origin facility → motor carrier/driver → receiver/destination facility**

A broker may have arranged the transportation, and a dispatcher may coordinate carrier-side activity, but neither fact means that the broker or dispatcher physically transported the freight.

### Flow 2 — Information and communication
Information moves differently from freight.

Examples include:
- pickup and delivery addresses;
- appointment times;
- commodity description;
- equipment requirement;
- weight and dimensions;
- driver/truck status;
- reference or pickup numbers;
- contact information;
- delay or exception notices;
- delivery status.

The dispatcher often sits near the center of this information flow on the carrier side. That does not mean the dispatcher owns every decision or creates every fact. It means the dispatcher must know what information is needed, where it came from, who needs it, and whether it is reliable enough to use.

### Flow 3 — Documents
Documents have their own lifecycle.

At an introductory level, recognize these common documents:

**Rate confirmation** — commonly records agreed load-specific commercial and operating information in brokered freight. Later modules address booking, terms, discrepancies, and document review in greater depth.

**Bill of lading (BOL)** — commonly accompanies the freight movement and records shipment information. The precise legal and operational significance can depend on context; at this stage, learn to recognize it as a core shipment document.

**Proof of delivery (POD)** — delivery documentation showing that the freight was delivered/received, commonly a signed delivery copy of a transportation document or other accepted delivery evidence.

**Invoice** — the carrier's request for payment for transportation services after the required billing conditions are satisfied.

A dispatcher should not collapse these into “paperwork.” Each document answers different questions and appears at a different point in the workflow.

### Flow 4 — Money and payment
Money does not travel with the truck.

A simplified brokered-load payment flow may look like:

**customer/shipper payment obligation → broker payment process → carrier invoice/payment → factor involvement if the carrier factors the receivable**

That is a teaching model, not a universal payment chain. Direct-customer arrangements, factoring structures, payment terms, assignments, and contractual relationships can differ. The important Module 01 lesson is that the party arranging freight, the party moving freight, and the party financing an invoice are different functions.

### Why separating the four flows matters
Suppose the truck has delivered the freight but the signed delivery document has not reached the billing process. The **physical freight flow may be complete while the document and payment flows are not**.

Suppose a broker emails a pickup change but the driver has not received it. The information exists, but the **communication flow has failed**.

Suppose a dispatcher has a rate confirmation but does not yet have the pickup reference required by the facility. A document is present, but the **operating information is incomplete**.

Professional dispatching requires seeing these differences.

### Desk Drill 1 — Separate the flows
For each event, identify the primary flow involved: PHYSICAL, INFORMATION, DOCUMENT, or PAYMENT. Some events may affect more than one flow; choose the primary one and explain the secondary effect.

1. Driver checks in at the consignee.
2. Dispatcher sends a revised appointment time to the driver.
3. Signed delivery paperwork is returned after unloading.
4. Carrier submits its billing package.
5. Factor advances funds on an eligible carrier receivable.
6. Freight is transferred from the origin dock into the trailer.

## 2. What a basic dispatch file must tell you

Before a dispatcher can make a useful decision, the file needs enough information to describe the movement and the carrier resources being considered.

### Carrier and driver context
At foundation level, ask:
- Which motor carrier is involved?
- Which truck/driver is being considered?
- Where is the truck now?
- When is the truck expected to be available?
- What equipment is available?
- Are there obvious stated carrier or driver restrictions?

Module 01 does not teach you to perform the full equipment-fit, driver-qualification, onboarding, or hours-of-service analysis. It teaches you to recognize that those facts exist and that they matter.

### Freight context
Ask:
- What is the commodity or general freight description?
- Where is pickup?
- Where is delivery?
- What are the pickup and delivery dates/times or windows?
- What equipment is requested?
- What weight/dimensions are stated?
- Are special instructions stated?

A field appearing on a load record does not automatically make the information complete or correct.

### Commercial context
At introductory level, recognize:
- stated rate or compensation field;
- who is offering/tendering the opportunity;
- who the carrier-side contact is communicating with;
- whether a commercial document has been issued.

Do not jump ahead into profitability or negotiation. Module 07 teaches advanced profitability mathematics; Module 08 teaches negotiation.

### Status context
A load can be in different operating states:
- opportunity identified;
- information being gathered;
- verification pending;
- accepted/booked under the applicable workflow;
- pickup pending;
- at pickup;
- in transit;
- at delivery;
- delivered;
- documents pending;
- billing/payment handoff.

These labels are operational examples, not a universal industry status code. A carrier, TMS, broker, or customer may use different terminology.

## 3. Foundational operating vocabulary in context

Definitions become useful only when they change what you understand about the load.

### Appointment
A scheduled pickup or delivery time, or a defined appointment window. If the file says “delivery appointment 08:00,” the dispatcher should not treat that facility as though arrival at any time is equivalent.

### FCFS — First Come, First Served
A facility may receive trucks in arrival order during stated receiving hours rather than assigning each truck a unique appointment. FCFS does not mean “arrive whenever you want.” Facility hours, cutoff rules, queues, and other requirements can still matter.

### Live load / live unload
The driver remains with the equipment while the facility loads or unloads it. This can affect time at the facility and the driver's operating day.

### Drop-and-hook
A driver drops one trailer and hooks to another rather than remaining for the entire loading/unloading process. Actual procedures vary by facility and carrier.

### Loaded miles
Miles traveled while moving the load. Later profitability work will use mileage more rigorously.

### Deadhead
Miles traveled without the revenue load aboard, often to reach pickup or reposition after delivery. Deadhead is not automatically “bad”; it is an operating fact that later modules evaluate in context.

### Lane
A recurring or described freight movement between geographic markets or origin/destination areas. “Dallas–Houston” can describe a lane even though individual pickups and deliveries occur at specific facilities.

### Capacity
Available transportation capability. Depending on context, people may be talking about trucks, trailers, drivers, equipment types, or broader market supply. Do not assume the word means exactly the same thing in every conversation.

### Desk Drill 2 — Translate the shorthand
A load note reads:

> Pickup: Fort Worth, TX — FCFS 07:00–14:00  
> Delivery: Little Rock, AR — appointment 09:00 next day  
> Equipment: 53' dry van  
> Driver empty in Dallas 05:30  
> Live load / live unload

Explain in plain language:
1. What timing information is known?
2. What does FCFS change about how you read pickup?
3. What does the delivery appointment tell you?
4. Where does deadhead appear in this file?
5. What does “live load/live unload” tell you — and what does it **not** tell you?

## 4. Known, unknown, and verify

A professional beginner must become comfortable saying **I do not have enough information yet**.

Use three categories:

### KNOWN
The file explicitly states the fact, and you understand what the statement says.

Example: `Pickup: Fort Worth, TX.`

### UNKNOWN
The information is absent.

Example: the load posting gives a city and weight but no commodity.

### VERIFY
Information is present, but operational reliance requires confirmation, reconciliation, or a more authoritative source.

Example: a copied load note shows a pickup time that conflicts with a newer message.

**Present does not always mean verified.**

The correct response to missing or conflicting information is not to invent the most likely answer.

### Desk Drill 3 — File triage
A dispatcher receives this fictional note:

> “Van load — Dallas to Memphis — 40,000 lb — pickup tomorrow — pays $1,700 — call Mike.”

Classify each item as KNOWN, UNKNOWN, or VERIFY:
- origin city;
- exact pickup facility;
- exact pickup time/window;
- destination city;
- exact delivery facility;
- delivery appointment/window;
- stated weight;
- commodity;
- required trailer details;
- identity/role of “Mike”;
- whether the opportunity is still available;
- whether the stated rate belongs to the current opportunity.

Then list the first five questions you would need answered before treating this as a usable freight file.

## 5. The Module 01 decision framework

Use this whenever a basic freight file is confusing or incomplete.

### STOP
Do not fill gaps with assumptions merely to keep the process moving.

### IDENTIFY
Identify:
- parties;
- carrier/driver/equipment context;
- freight;
- places;
- times;
- documents;
- stated commercial information;
- current status.

### TRACE
Trace what is happening in each flow:
- physical freight;
- information;
- documents;
- payment.

### VERIFY
Mark what is missing, conflicting, stale, unconfirmed, or outside the evidence currently available.

### HAND OFF
Recognize when the question belongs to a specialized workflow or authorized decision-maker rather than guessing.

Examples:
- Is this dispatch arrangement crossing the broker/dispatcher legal boundary? → specialized legal-boundary analysis.
- Will this freight safely and legally fit the available equipment? → equipment/freight-fit workflow.
- Is the carrier/driver onboarding file sufficient? → onboarding workflow.
- Is this opportunity commercially profitable? → profitability workflow.
- Can the trip legally be completed within applicable HOS constraints? → later trip-planning/HOS workflow.

Module 01 gives you the discipline to recognize the question. Later modules teach you how to answer it.

## 6. Freight File Lab — four different operating pictures

All names and details below are fictional training data.

### File A — Brokered interstate movement
**Prairie Freight Brokers** offers **North Star Transport** a dry-van shipment from Omaha, Nebraska to Des Moines, Iowa. North Star's dispatcher coordinates carrier-side communication. A North Star driver physically moves the freight. Delivery paperwork is returned after delivery, and North Star later submits its billing package.

Identify:
1. Who arranged the transportation?
2. Who transported the freight?
3. Who coordinates on the carrier side?
4. Which event completes the physical flow?
5. Which event begins the post-delivery document/payment handoff?

### File B — Direct customer movement
**Red Mesa Manufacturing** contacts **Canyon Line Transport** directly about moving packaged components from its plant to a customer distribution center. No broker is identified in the file.

What changes from File A?
- Do not invent a broker merely because many freight movements use brokers.
- Identify which functions still must occur even though the party structure is different.
- List the information Canyon Line's dispatcher would still need before the freight file is operationally useful.

### File C — Truck stays in one state
A truck picks up in Houston, Texas and delivers in Dallas, Texas. The file does not state the shipment's larger origin, destination, or intended continuous movement.

Can you conclude from those two cities alone that the transportation is legally intrastate for every regulatory purpose?

**No.** The truck's physical path is an important fact, but interstate/intrastate analysis can depend on the broader movement and applicable legal context. Module 01 requires you to identify the classification question rather than invent facts that are not in the file.

### File D — Incomplete opportunity
**Metro Logistics Contact:** “Need a 53' van tomorrow. 44,000 lb. Oklahoma City to Nashville. Good rate. Driver needs to be there early.”

Before doing anything else, apply:

**STOP → IDENTIFY → TRACE → VERIFY → HAND OFF**

Write:
- five facts you can identify;
- eight missing or verification-needed facts;
- which later specialized analyses may eventually be required;
- why “good rate” is not enough information to determine whether the opportunity is good for the carrier.

## 7. What good foundational dispatch thinking sounds like

Weak: **“It says Dallas to Memphis, so I know the load.”**

Stronger: **“I know the city pair, but I still need facilities, timing, commodity, equipment requirements, status, and verified contacts before this becomes a usable freight file.”**

Weak: **“The truck stays in Texas, so it's intrastate.”**

Stronger: **“The truck movement shown is within Texas, but I do not have enough information to make every interstate/intrastate legal classification from geography alone.”**

Weak: **“There is a rate confirmation, so everything is good.”**

Stronger: **“I recognize the document, but document presence does not by itself establish that every fact is current, correct, authentic, or operationally complete.”**

Weak: **“The broker moved the load.”**

Stronger: **“The broker arranged transportation; the motor carrier, through its driver/equipment, performed the motor transportation in this scenario.”**

Weak: **“I don't know, but it's probably fine.”**

Professional foundation: **“I don't have enough verified information to rely on that yet.”**

## 8. Module 01 operating standard

Before leaving this module, you should be able to look at a simple freight file and answer:

1. **Who are the parties?**
2. **Who is performing each function?**
3. **Where is the freight physically going?**
4. **What information is moving, and who needs it?**
5. **What documents should I recognize at this stage?**
6. **Where is the payment/billing handoff in the simplified lifecycle?**
7. **What do I know?**
8. **What is missing?**
9. **What needs verification?**
10. **What happens next?**
11. **What question belongs to a later specialized workflow?**

That is the foundation of dispatcher judgment. The rest of the Academy builds on it.
